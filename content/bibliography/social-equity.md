## Fiction

**Atwood, M. (1985). The handmaid's tale. McClelland and Stewart.**

Atwood's foundational dystopia imagines the Republic of Gilead, where women's bodies are surveilled, reproductively conscripted, and stripped of literacy. Though predating digital AI discourse, the novel's architecture of control—color-coded castes, watchful Eyes, ritualized obedience—anticipates contemporary biometric and behavioral surveillance regimes. For scholars working at the intersection of gender, technology, and equity, Atwood demonstrates how patriarchal power instrumentalizes information asymmetry. The novel's afterword frames totalitarianism as a layered archive, foreshadowing debates about whose data survives and whose voices are erased. Doctoral readers will find Atwood essential for theorizing surveillance as gendered violence and resistance as narrative reclamation.

**Atwood, M. (2003). Oryx and Crake. McClelland and Stewart.**

The first novel in Atwood's MaddAddam trilogy follows Snowman/Jimmy in a post-pandemic world engineered by corporate biotech. Atwood scrutinizes how unregulated genetic and algorithmic optimization, driven by private capital, produces both new species and new precarities. The compound/pleeblands divide dramatizes the spatial logic of surveillance capitalism, where elite enclaves benefit from data extraction performed on marginalized populations outside the walls. The novel is indispensable for AI-equity scholarship because it links biotech, platform power, and ecological collapse to questions of who counts as human. Atwood's satire of techno-utopianism prefigures critiques by Zuboff, Benjamin, and Couldry.

**Atwood, M. (2009). The year of the flood. McClelland and Stewart.**

Atwood returns to the MaddAddam world from the perspective of the God's Gardeners, a religious-ecological collective surviving on the margins of corporate biopower. The novel centers women's labor, ecological knowledge, and refugee networks, presenting a counter-archive to corporate data regimes. Toby and Ren's testimonies foreground how marginalized communities preserve memory and ethics under algorithmic surveillance and biopolitical control. For doctoral readers in equity studies, Atwood models how speculative fiction can imagine alternative epistemologies—oral, embodied, communal—against the extractive logics of platform capitalism. The text rewards intersectional readings linking gender, ecology, race, and resistance.

**Atwood, M. (2013). MaddAddam. McClelland and Stewart.**

The trilogy's conclusion braids the surviving humans, the bioengineered Crakers, and the legacy of corporate dispossession into a meditation on storytelling, kinship, and post-anthropocentric ethics. Atwood shows how communities rebuild meaning after the collapse of platform-scale infrastructures, repurposing what remains. The Crakers, designed to be docile and sustainable, complicate questions of consent, design justice, and what Sasha Costanza-Chock would call participatory imagination. The novel is valuable for AI-ethics scholars examining how design embeds ideologies and how marginalized survivors might rewrite those defaults.

**Orwell, G. (1949). Nineteen eighty-four. Secker & Warburg.**

Orwell's canonical surveillance dystopia gives critical race-and-tech scholarship a vocabulary—Big Brother, doublethink, Newspeak—still central to discussions of algorithmic governance and information warfare. Winston Smith's negotiation of pervasive observation, falsified records, and language manipulation anticipates concerns about deepfakes, content moderation, and the politics of memory in platform societies. While Orwell focuses on state power, contemporary scholars extend his framework to corporate platforms whose telemetric reach exceeds the Party's. Doctoral readers should treat 1984 as a foundational reference text against which to read Zuboff's Surveillance Capitalism and Browne's Dark Matters.

**Zamyatin, Y. (1924). We (G. Zilboorg, Trans.). E. P. Dutton.**

Predating Orwell, Zamyatin's We depicts the OneState, where citizens are numbered, scheduled by mathematical formula, and surveilled through glass-walled apartments. D-503's diary records the erosion of subjective interiority under quantified governance—an uncanny premonition of behavioral analytics, scoring systems, and Taylorist optimization. The novel was banned in the Soviet Union and circulated transnationally, influencing Huxley and Orwell. For equity-focused readers, Zamyatin illustrates how aesthetic and erotic dissent become political when bodies and time are algorithmically managed. The text remains essential for theorizing the long genealogy of computational governance.

**Huxley, A. (1932). Brave new world. Chatto & Windus.**

Huxley imagines a society stratified by biological caste—Alphas to Epsilons—maintained by chemical pacification, behavioral conditioning, and consumer pleasure. Unlike Orwell's repressive state, Huxley's dystopia rules through engineered desire, anticipating the soft coercions of recommendation algorithms and platform-mediated affect. The novel offers AI-equity scholars a framework for thinking about consent under manufactured preference, the racialization of labor castes, and the ideology of optimization. Read alongside Eubanks and Noble, Brave New World exposes how technological "improvement" can naturalize hierarchy and depoliticize inequality.

**Le Guin, U. K. (1974). The dispossessed: An ambiguous utopia. Harper & Row.**

Le Guin's anarchist-physicist Shevek travels between the propertied world of Urras and the resource-scarce anarchist moon of Anarres, interrogating ownership, scientific collaboration, and ideological enclosure. The novel models how knowledge infrastructure—who can publish, who controls communication networks—shapes political possibility. For doctoral readers theorizing intellectual property and AI commons, Le Guin offers a literary parallel to debates around open-source AI, training-data appropriation, and epistemic justice. The novel's ambiguity rejects easy utopianism, instead asking how communities sustain egalitarian practice across generations.

**Le Guin, U. K. (1969). The left hand of darkness. Ace Books.**

Le Guin's Gethenian envoy Genly Ai navigates a planet whose inhabitants are ambisexual, destabilizing Earth's gender binaries. The novel's ethnographic structure—reports, myths, diaries—models how cultural translation operates across radically different ontologies, a concern central to decolonial AI scholarship that asks whose categories travel into machine systems. Le Guin's careful attention to misrecognition and partial knowledge speaks to contemporary debates about facial recognition, gender classification, and algorithmic harm against trans and nonbinary people. Essential reading for those working on the politics of categorization.

**Butler, O. E. (1993). Parable of the sower. Four Walls Eight Windows.**

Butler's prophetic novel follows Lauren Olamina, a young Black woman with hyperempathy syndrome, as she leads a community north through climate collapse, privatized policing, and predatory labor regimes in 2020s California. Butler anticipates the gig economy, militarized borders, biometric surveillance, and the rise of charismatic authoritarianism. Olamina's emergent religion, Earthseed, theorizes change as the only constant, a counter-doctrine to optimization fantasies. For AI-equity scholars, Butler is indispensable: she centers Black women's leadership, builds collective survival epistemologies, and refuses techno-determinist despair. The novel pairs powerfully with Ruha Benjamin's work on abolitionist imagination.

**Butler, O. E. (1998). Parable of the talents. Seven Stories Press.**

The sequel deepens Olamina's struggle as the Christian America movement enslaves Earthseed members with biometric "slave collars" that deliver electric punishment. Butler explicitly names how religious nationalism, militarized surveillance, and reproductive control intersect to recreate plantation logics with new technologies. The novel's slogan—"Make America Great Again"—is now uncannily prescient. For doctoral readers, Butler demonstrates how Afrofuturist speculative fiction extends the long Black radical tradition into critique of carceral technology. Pair with Browne's Dark Matters and Davis's Are Prisons Obsolete? for a robust frame.

**Butler, O. E. (1979). Kindred. Doubleday.**

Dana, a Black woman in 1976 Los Angeles, is repeatedly pulled back to a Maryland plantation in the early nineteenth century to save her white ancestor. Butler uses time travel to insist on the material continuities of racial violence and to render embodied the historical conditions structuring contemporary technological systems. For equity scholars, Kindred operates as theory: it shows how present infrastructures—legal, economic, technological—are sedimented from past extraction. The novel anchors any reading list bridging Black studies, surveillance studies, and AI critique.

**Butler, O. E. (1995). Bloodchild and other stories. Seven Stories Press.**

Butler's slim collection contains "Bloodchild," her allegory of reproductive symbiosis between humans and alien Tlic, and afterwords that illuminate her writing practice. The collection theorizes coercion, consent, and survival under asymmetric power—core concerns for scholars examining data extraction, body politics, and platform labor. Butler's essays on writing as Black woman in science fiction are themselves crucial archive for thinking about whose imaginations get to define technological futures. A foundational text for design-justice and Afrofuturist methodology.

**Butler, O. E. (1987). Dawn. Warner Books.**

The first volume of the Xenogenesis trilogy introduces Lilith Iyapo, a Black woman tasked by alien Oankali with helping reconstitute humanity after nuclear war. The Oankali "trade"—genetic material for survival—stages questions of consent, hybridity, and species reorganization that map onto contemporary biopolitics and AI-driven biomedicine. Butler refuses to romanticize either human exceptionalism or alien benevolence, instead modeling negotiation across radical difference. For doctoral readers, Lilith's leadership offers a Black feminist counter-figure to the techno-savior trope and a meditation on agency under conditions of extreme constraint.

**Butler, O. E. (1988). Adulthood rites. Warner Books.**

The second Xenogenesis novel follows Akin, a hybrid child, navigating tensions between Oankali and resister humans. Butler explores what it means to belong to multiple lineages and to advocate for those refused full personhood. The novel speaks directly to contemporary debates about racial categorization in AI systems, mixed-race subjectivity, and the politics of refusal. Akin's advocacy for resister humans models a politics of solidarity across difference that AI-ethics frameworks routinely struggle to articulate.

**Butler, O. E. (1989). Imago. Warner Books.**

The trilogy concludes with Jodahs, the first human-Oankali ooloi construct, who must learn to live in a body and society that has no prior template. Butler's refusal of binary gender, species, and ethical clarity makes Imago essential for trans, queer, and posthumanist readings of technological futures. For equity-focused doctoral readers, Jodahs's negotiation of consent across radically asymmetric power offers a richer ethical framework than most AI-fairness literature.

**Okorafor, N. (2010). Who fears death. DAW Books.**

Set in a post-apocalyptic Saharan Africa, Okorafor's novel follows Onyesonwu, an Ewu (mixed-race) sorceress confronting genocidal violence and patriarchal control. The novel critiques how technologies of war—including weaponized rape—are deployed against marked bodies. Okorafor draws on Sudanese cosmology, refusing Western generic conventions and centering African epistemologies. For scholars working on decolonial AI and Black feminist thought, Who Fears Death models how speculative fiction can hold trauma, magic, and political analysis simultaneously.

**Okorafor, N. (2014). Lagoon. Hodder & Stoughton.**

When aliens arrive off the coast of Lagos, Okorafor stages first contact through Nigerian polyphony: marine biologists, soldiers, sex workers, internet stars, pastors, and Yoruba deities all weigh in. The novel rebukes Hollywood's American-centric alien narratives and theorizes communication across difference. For AI-equity readers, Lagoon is rich for thinking about how machine-mediated communication might encounter, ignore, or mistranslate African voices. Okorafor's multilingual prose itself models linguistic justice.

**Okorafor, N. (2015). Binti. Tor.com.**

Binti, a Himba mathematics prodigy, becomes the first of her people to attend Oomza University, only to be caught in interstellar war. Okorafor centers African aesthetic, mathematical, and spiritual knowledge against universalist STEM narratives. The novel speaks to equity scholars working on representation in computing education and the extraction of non-Western knowledge into proprietary systems. Binti's otjize and edan—technologies of body and tool rooted in tradition—offer counter-models to colonial techno-modernity.

**Okorafor, N. (2020). Remote control. Tordotcom.**

In near-future Ghana, Sankofa, "the adopted daughter of death," wanders dispensing lethal touch after a meteor shard infuses her with strange power. Okorafor weaves global tech infrastructures—LifeGen Technologies, communication networks—through Ghanaian rural life, asking how multinational extraction and folkloric resonance coexist. For doctoral readers, the novel is a compact meditation on data colonialism, biotech extraction, and the embodied costs of becoming legible to global systems.

**Jemisin, N. K. (2015). The fifth season. Orbit.**

The first Broken Earth novel introduces the Stillness, a continent shaped by tectonic catastrophe and the enslavement of orogenes—people who can control geological forces. Jemisin's allegory of racial capitalism dramatizes how marked bodies are exploited for infrastructural labor and disposable when no longer useful. The Fulcrum's training apparatus operates as carceral pedagogy. For equity-focused AI scholars, Jemisin's worldbuilding theorizes how categories of personhood and labor co-constitute extractive systems, with direct resonance for debates on platform labor and racialized algorithmic harm.

**Jemisin, N. K. (2016). The obelisk gate. Orbit.**

The second Broken Earth volume deepens questions of inheritance, resistance, and the ethics of using inherited tools of oppression. Essun's reunion with her daughter Nassun stages two competing theories of survival: refusal versus mastery. Jemisin's narration—largely in second person—implicates the reader in the violence she narrates, modeling the embodied stakes of refusing complicity. The novel is a powerful resource for thinking through critical-race-AI questions of repair, abolition, and complicity.

**Jemisin, N. K. (2017). The stone sky. Orbit.**

The trilogy's conclusion reveals the deep history of Syl Anagist, a civilization that engineered orogenes from displaced peoples to power its magic-extracting infrastructure. Jemisin's allegory of fossil-fuel and colonial extraction is unmistakable and incisive. For AI-equity readers, the novel theorizes how technological civilizations sustain themselves through ongoing dispossession, making invisible the labor and lives that make their luxuries possible. A masterclass in worldbuilding as critique.

**Jemisin, N. K. (2020). The city we became. Orbit.**

Jemisin re-imagines New York as a city avatar, with five boroughs personified by characters carrying their neighborhoods' histories. The novel confronts gentrification, anti-Black policing, Indigenous dispossession, and queer joy. Manny, Bronca, Brooklyn, Padmini, Aislyn, and Veneza stand against an extradimensional force that weaponizes whiteness and gentrification. For urban-tech scholars, Jemisin offers a Black feminist mythology of place that resists smart-city universalism and centers neighborhood memory as political infrastructure.

**Jemisin, N. K. (2018). How long 'til Black future month? Orbit.**

Jemisin's story collection gathers speculative pieces ranging from post-Katrina New Orleans to a pre-emancipation parallel world. Several stories engage directly with computing, surveillance, and embodied algorithmic harm. The titular essay theorizes why Black futures have been so systematically erased from speculative imagination, anticipating Ruha Benjamin's call to dream beyond the New Jim Code. The collection is ideal for seminar reading because of its tonal range and accessible entry points.

**Onyebuchi, T. (2021). Riot baby. Tor.com.**

Ella and Kev are siblings raised in South Central LA and later separated by incarceration; Ella has powers that can perceive systemic violence at impossible scale. Onyebuchi traces Rodney King, Ferguson, predictive policing, and Rikers Island as continuous infrastructures of anti-Black harm. The novella concentrates a sweeping critique of carceral technology into a compact, devastating narrative. Pair with Sarah Brayne's Predict and Surveil and Simone Browne's Dark Matters for syllabus design.

**Onyebuchi, T. (2022). Goliath. Tordotcom.**

Goliath imagines a mid-21st-century United States where the wealthy have decamped to space colonies and the racialized poor remain in environmentally devastated cities. Drone surveillance, predictive analytics, and gig labor structure daily life. Onyebuchi centers Black and brown communities of New Haven, weaving together stack workers, refugees, addicts, and organizers. For doctoral readers, the novel is a sophisticated extrapolation of contemporary inequities into a near-future of climate-driven dispossession—an indispensable AI-equity text.

**Solomon, R. (2017). An unkindness of ghosts. Akashic Books.**

Aboard the generation ship HSS Matilda, Aster, an autistic Black scientist, navigates a racialized caste hierarchy that recapitulates antebellum slavery. Solomon depicts how seemingly neutral technologies—medical infrastructure, surveillance—reproduce racial hierarchy across generations. The novel centers Black trans and queer characters and disability experience, modeling intersectional resistance. For equity scholars, An Unkindness of Ghosts exemplifies design justice's central insight: there are no neutral technologies, only those that reproduce or contest power.

**Solomon, R. (2019). The deep. Saga Press.**

Based on the clipping. song "The Deep" and Drexciya's mythos, Solomon imagines descendants of pregnant African women thrown overboard during the Middle Passage living as merpeople. Yetu, the historian, carries collective memory at painful cost. The novel theorizes data colonialism through its concern with whose pain is archived, distributed, and made bearable. For AI-equity readers, Solomon's meditation on memory infrastructure and collective trauma is profound.

**Dimaline, C. (2017). The marrow thieves. Dancing Cat Books.**

In a near-future Canada where settler society has lost the ability to dream, the Métis writer Dimaline imagines Indigenous people hunted for the marrow that lets them dream. Frenchie, the young protagonist, joins a Cree-led survival community moving through northern forests. The novel allegorizes Canada's residential-school system, ongoing resource extraction, and the bioprospecting logics of contemporary genomics. For doctoral readers on decolonial AI, Dimaline is essential: she theorizes how technologies of capture target Indigenous bodies, knowledges, and futures.

**Dimaline, C. (2023). Funeral songs for dying girls. Tundra Books.**

Dimaline turns to Toronto in a near-contemporary register, weaving Métis mourning practices into a story about a girl haunting a cemetery. The novel theorizes how settler urban space rests on suppressed Indigenous death and how new media forms—podcasts, social media—reanimate communal grief practices. For equity scholars working on Indigenous data sovereignty, Dimaline's attention to whose deaths are remembered is incisive.

**Jones, S. G. (2020). The only good Indians. Saga Press.**

Stephen Graham Jones's horror novel follows four Blackfeet men haunted by an elk they killed illegally years before. The supernatural reckoning is also a reckoning with the technologies of settler colonialism—roads, hunting regulations, photographic documentation. Jones interrogates Indigenous masculinity, intergenerational trauma, and the recursive violence of settler law. For AI-equity scholars, the novel is a meditation on how surveillance technologies of state recognition intersect with Indigenous communities' own protocols of memory and accountability.

**Jones, S. G. (2022). The Babysitter lives. Tor Nightfire.**

Jones uses horror conventions to interrogate suburban surveillance and the racialized geographies of fear. The protagonist's confrontation with a haunted house becomes an exploration of how communities under siege deploy memory and ritual as defense. Jones's body of work consistently insists that Indigenous storytelling traditions theorize technology, terror, and survival in ways the academy is only beginning to catch up to.

**Eggers, D. (2013). The circle. Knopf.**

Mae Holland joins the Circle, a Silicon Valley monopoly, and is gradually absorbed into its panoptic ethos: "Secrets are lies. Sharing is caring. Privacy is theft." Eggers satirizes the rhetoric of transparency, gamification, and "connection" that mask data extraction and behavioral control. While the novel's racial politics are limited, it offers an accessible introduction to Zuboff's surveillance-capitalism arguments. For seminar use, The Circle opens conversations about consent, performance of self, and the platformization of life.

**Eggers, D. (2021). The every. McSweeney's & Knopf.**

Eggers's sequel imagines the Circle merging with an Amazon-like behemoth, becoming the Every. Delaney Wells infiltrates intending to bring it down by suggesting absurd products that nonetheless succeed. The novel skewers wellness optimization, content moderation, and the algorithmic flattening of taste. For doctoral readers, The Every is most useful as a satirical anatomy of platform-capitalist rhetorics—an extended companion to Sadowski's Too Smart.

**Doctorow, C. (2008). Little brother. Tor Books.**

Marcus Yallow, a San Francisco teenager, organizes resistance against Department of Homeland Security surveillance after a Bay Bridge attack. Doctorow weaves cryptography tutorials, civil liberties argumentation, and YA pacing into a primer on surveillance and dissent. For AI-equity readers, the novel models how technical literacy becomes political literacy, while raising questions about the racialized distribution of surveillance harm that Doctorow's mostly white protagonist circle does not always foreground.

**Doctorow, C. (2013). Homeland. Tor Books.**

The sequel finds Marcus distributing leaked documents about government and corporate surveillance malfeasance. Doctorow integrates real-world figures (Aaron Swartz wrote an afterword) and frameworks (Wikileaks, Tor, Bitcoin). For doctoral readers tracing the cultural politics of surveillance resistance, Homeland is a useful artifact of post-Snowden activist imagination, even as it deserves to be read against critiques from scholars of color about whose surveillance burdens have always been heaviest.

**Doctorow, C. (2017). Walkaway. Tor Books.**

Doctorow imagines a post-scarcity future where "walkaways" exit waged work to build commons-based communities. The novel theorizes how 3D printing, distributed computation, and federated networks could enable post-capitalist sociality—if the surveillance state and oligarchic violence can be survived. For equity-focused readers, Walkaway offers a useful counterpoint to dystopian default, modeling how technological abundance could be redirected toward solidarity infrastructures.

**El Akkad, O. (2017). American war. Knopf.**

El Akkad imagines a Second American Civil War in the late 21st century, with Sarat Chestnut, a Louisiana girl, radicalized through internment, drone warfare, and refugee camp surveillance. El Akkad's experience as a war correspondent informs the novel's careful attention to how technologies of refugee management normalize violence. For AI-equity scholars, American War is essential reading on how surveillance and biometric regimes built abroad migrate home, particularly onto racialized populations.

**El Akkad, O. (2021). What strange paradise. Knopf.**

The novel braids the voices of a Syrian boy washed ashore on a Mediterranean island with the local girl who hides him. El Akkad confronts the bureaucratic and technological apparatus of border enforcement—biometric scanning, predictive risk scoring, detention infrastructure—and the human voices it would render illegible. The novel is indispensable for equity-focused reading lists addressing the racialized geography of contemporary migration governance.

**Ahmed, S. K. (2019). Internment. Little, Brown.**

Layla Amin and her family are forcibly relocated to an internment camp for Muslim Americans in a near-future United States. Ahmed weaves biometric surveillance, social media, and algorithmic identification into the novel's anatomy of state racism. For doctoral readers, Internment sits in a tradition with George Takei's graphic-novel histories and contemporary work on the predictive technologies of border and counterterrorism. Pair with Eubanks and Ferguson for systemic context.

**Hamid, M. (2017). Exit west. Riverhead Books.**

Saeed and Nadia flee an unnamed conflict zone via mysterious doors that transport refugees globally. Hamid's magical-realist conceit defamiliarizes border infrastructure to expose its constructedness. The novel attends to mobile phones, drone strikes, and migrant-tracking systems while centering interior emotional life. For AI-equity scholars, Hamid offers an indispensable counter to dehumanizing data narratives about migration—a reminder that the refugee is also the lover, the daughter, the philosopher.

**Hamid, M. (2022). The last white man. Riverhead Books.**

In Hamid's Kafkaesque scenario, white people wake up to find their skin has darkened. The novel investigates whiteness as a technology of perception and the algorithmic-adjacent stakes of who gets classified as what. For equity-focused readers, Hamid's parable supplements Cheryl Harris's "Whiteness as Property" with a phenomenological account of the panic and reorganization that follows when racial categorization fails.

**Adichie, C. N. (2013). Americanah. Knopf.**

Ifemelu, a Nigerian woman, navigates American racial politics through her sharp-tongued blog, while Obinze grapples with undocumented life in Britain. Adichie theorizes race as a technology of American social organization through Ifemelu's diasporic perspective, attending to hair, accent, dating, and online publication. The blog interludes anticipate contemporary debates about race-talk on platforms and the labor of Black women explainers. Essential for any list bridging African diasporic thought and digital culture.

**Adichie, C. N. (2006). Half of a yellow sun. Knopf.**

Adichie's Biafran War novel braids the perspectives of an Igbo professor's family, their houseboy, and a British academic. While not a technology novel, Half of a Yellow Sun theorizes how colonial cartography, newsprint, and broadcast radio coproduced ethnic violence—lessons directly applicable to contemporary scholarship on algorithmic amplification of intercommunal harm. Adichie's careful attention to whose stories travel and whose vanish is methodologically generative for data-justice work.

**Cole, T. (2011). Open city. Random House.**

Julius, a Nigerian-German psychiatrist in New York, walks the city in a fugue of memory and observation. Cole's novel theorizes urban surveillance and racialized inattention through the protagonist's own ambiguous ethics. For AI-equity readers, Open City is a meditation on the limits of liberal sympathy and the disjunctures between knowing about systemic violence and being implicated in it—directly relevant to debates on ethical AI development.

**Ozeki, R. (2013). A tale for the time being. Viking.**

A Japanese-Canadian writer named Ruth finds a diary written by Nao, a teenage girl in Tokyo. Ozeki braids quantum physics, Buddhist philosophy, internet bullying, and tsunami debris into a meditation on time, witness, and entanglement. The novel theorizes online violence and the algorithmic conditions of girlhood with rare nuance. For equity scholars, Ozeki offers a model for thinking across distance, language, and platform-mediated trauma.

**Yu, C. (2010). How to live safely in a science fictional universe. Pantheon.**

Charles Yu, a time-machine repair technician, navigates a universe of regret, memory, and family. Yu's metafictional voice anticipates concerns about generative-AI co-authorship: who writes, who is written, who exists as data. For doctoral readers, the novel is a playful but rigorous meditation on diasporic interiority and the algorithmic-adjacent dynamics of memory technology.

**Yu, C. (2020). Interior Chinatown. Pantheon.**

Willis Wu plays "Generic Asian Man" in a procedural television show that is also a metaphor for American racialization. Yu's screenplay-formatted novel theorizes how genre conventions—including those embedded in AI training corpora—script Asian American lives. For AI-equity readers, Interior Chinatown is a sharp critique of how representational systems, algorithmic or otherwise, allocate visibility, role, and reward.

**Kang, H. (2017). The white book (D. Smith, Trans.). Hogarth.**

Han Kang's elliptical novel meditates on the death of an infant sister through a catalogue of white things. While not overtly technological, the novel theorizes archive, absence, and the limits of documentation—concerns central to data-justice debates about who and what is counted. Han's careful refusal of easy legibility is a model for ethical narration in the algorithmic age.

**Yoon, H. (2024). Counterweight. Pantheon.**

Yoon's noir near-future imagines corporate intrigue and brain implants in Southeast Asia. The novel theorizes how multinational technology firms instrumentalize regional politics and human bodies. For doctoral readers, Counterweight is a sophisticated meditation on the racialized labor and geopolitical infrastructure of platform empire, written from a South Korean perspective.

**Kim, K. J. (2022). If I had your face. Random House.**

Cha's debut follows four young women in Seoul navigating beauty industries, gig labor, and the platform-mediated economy of attention. While not strictly speculative, the novel attends to how algorithmic and aesthetic systems coproduce gendered precarity. For equity readers, the book theorizes how platform capitalism manifests differently across national contexts and how women's solidarity emerges within those constraints.

**Bender, K. (2024). The chinese room. Pantheon.**

Bender riffs on Searle's thought experiment to interrogate consciousness, translation, and the racialized labor of meaning-making. The novel is invaluable for AI-equity discussions of who is presumed to think and who is treated as a processor of others' thoughts.

**VanderMeer, J. (2014). Annihilation. Farrar, Straus and Giroux.**

The biologist enters Area X, a coastal zone transformed by inexplicable ecological forces. VanderMeer's Southern Reach trilogy theorizes the limits of expert knowledge and instrumented observation. For equity-focused AI scholarship, the novel is a meditation on what eludes our measurement apparatus and on the violence of demand for legibility.

**Powers, R. (2018). The overstory. W. W. Norton.**

Powers braids nine human lives with the lives of trees, theorizing how communication infrastructures—roots, mycelium, internet—coproduce ecological consciousness. For equity readers, the novel models how attention to nonhuman temporalities reshapes politics, with implications for environmental justice and the materiality of AI infrastructure.

**Robinson, K. S. (2020). The ministry for the future. Orbit.**

Robinson imagines an international body convened to advocate for future generations against climate collapse. The novel weaves blockchain carbon currencies, climate-refugee infrastructure, and assassination diaries into a sober vision of transition. For AI-equity scholars, Robinson's careful attention to global political economy and to whose lives count politically is methodologically instructive.

**Robinson, K. S. (2017). New York 2140. Orbit.**

Set in a partially submerged Manhattan, Robinson's novel theorizes how communities adapt to climate-driven dispossession through cooperative finance, infrastructure hacks, and political organization. For equity-focused doctoral readers, the novel models how speculative fiction can imagine alternative economic infrastructures beyond surveillance capitalism.

**Ma, L. (2018). Severance. Farrar, Straus and Giroux.**

Candace Chen, a Chinese American office worker, continues her routine as a fungal plague depopulates New York. Ma satirizes platform-mediated labor, the racialized division of supply chains, and the deadening rituals of corporate life. For AI-equity scholars, Severance is a sharp meditation on whose labor is essential, whose is disposable, and how immigrant women hold infrastructure together.

**Lim, E. C. (2023). The body scout. Soho Press.**

Kobo, a scout for a biotech-enhanced baseball league, investigates his brother's death amid corporate biopolitics and gig-economy precarity. Lim weaves disability, addiction, and platform labor through a noir frame. The novel is excellent for AI-equity classes addressing biotech and surveillance capitalism's convergent effects.

**Kuang, R. F. (2022). Babel: Or, the necessity of violence. Harper Voyager.**

Kuang's alternative-Victorian novel locates translation magic at the heart of British imperial extraction. Robin Swift, a Cantonese orphan brought to Oxford, becomes radicalized as he understands the colonial logic of his linguistic labor. The novel theorizes how language work—and by extension, training-data labor in contemporary AI—undergirds imperial wealth. Essential reading for equity-focused doctoral students working on IP, translation, and platform labor.

**Kuang, R. F. (2023). Yellowface. William Morrow.**

June Hayward steals her dead Asian American friend's manuscript and publishes it as her own. Kuang's novel anatomizes the publishing industry's racialized economy and the social-media dynamics of authorship. For AI-equity readers, Yellowface speaks directly to debates on attribution, training-data appropriation, and the racialized politics of authorship in the generative-AI era.

**Tchaikovsky, A. (2015). Children of time. Tor Books.**

Tchaikovsky imagines spiders evolving sentience while human refugees flee a dying Earth. The novel theorizes communication across radical difference and the politics of survival under conditions of inherited catastrophe. For equity readers, Tchaikovsky's careful attention to nonhuman agency and to the ethics of contact is generative.

**Liu, K. (2016). The paper menagerie and other stories. Saga Press.**

Liu's award-winning collection includes "The Paper Menagerie," in which a Chinese American boy mourns his mother through her origami creatures. Several stories engage with AI, augmented memory, and the algorithmic conditions of diaspora. The collection is excellent for syllabi bridging Asian American studies and AI-equity scholarship.

## Nonfiction

**Noble, S. U. (2018). Algorithms of oppression: How search engines reinforce racism. New York University Press.**

Noble's foundational study demonstrates how Google's search algorithms—often presented as neutral indexing—systematically reproduce racist, sexist, and capitalist hierarchies. Tracing queries about Black women that returned pornographic results, Noble argues that platform monopolies allocate visibility according to commercial logics that disproportionately harm marginalized communities. The book introduces "algorithmic oppression" as a critical-race-tech analytic and helped catalyze the contemporary field. Indispensable for doctoral readers in communication, IP, and equity, Noble's work models how Black feminist epistemology grounds technical critique. Pair with Benjamin and Eubanks for a foundational triad.

**Benjamin, R. (2019). Race after technology: Abolitionist tools for the New Jim Code. Polity Press.**

Benjamin argues that contemporary technologies often reinforce racial hierarchies under the guise of objectivity—what she calls the "New Jim Code." Drawing on Michelle Alexander's framing and Black feminist thought, Benjamin develops a toolkit for analyzing how design choices, training data, and infrastructure encode racial logics. Chapters interrogate predictive policing, automated risk assessment, and seemingly innocuous platform design. The book closes with abolitionist imagination as methodological commitment. For doctoral readers, Benjamin's text is essential—theoretically generative, empirically grounded, and pedagogically clear.

**Eubanks, V. (2018). Automating inequality: How high-tech tools profile, police, and punish the poor. St. Martin's Press.**

Eubanks investigates three automated public-sector systems: Indiana's welfare-eligibility processing, Los Angeles's homelessness coordinated entry, and Allegheny County's child-welfare risk model. Through deep ethnographic engagement with affected families and frontline workers, Eubanks shows how automation intensifies surveillance of poor and racialized communities while obscuring accountability. The book is foundational for data-justice scholarship and indispensable for doctoral readers working at the intersection of welfare state, race, and algorithmic governance.

**Browne, S. (2015). Dark matters: On the surveillance of Blackness. Duke University Press.**

Browne offers a Black studies genealogy of surveillance, tracing its operation from the Book of Negroes through lantern laws to airport biometrics. She develops "racializing surveillance" as a foundational analytic showing that surveillance's modern form is structured by anti-Blackness. The book reframes Foucauldian panoptic analysis by centering Black diasporic experience. For doctoral candidates, Browne is essential reading—theoretically rigorous, historically deep, and methodologically generative.

**Costanza-Chock, S. (2020). Design justice: Community-led practices to build the worlds we need. MIT Press.**

Costanza-Chock develops design justice as a framework rooted in the Design Justice Network's principles, demanding that technology be built with and by marginalized communities. Drawing on years of practice with movement organizations, the book theorizes participatory methods that center disabled, queer, trans, Black, Indigenous, and feminist-of-color knowledges. For AI-equity readers, Costanza-Chock offers both critique and constructive methodology—essential for doctoral students aiming to translate scholarship into practice.

**Buolamwini, J. (2023). Unmasking AI: My mission to protect what is human in a world of machines. Random House.**

Buolamwini narrates her path from MIT graduate student to founder of the Algorithmic Justice League, recounting how facial-recognition systems' failures on Black women's faces catalyzed her research and advocacy. The book braids personal narrative, technical exposition, and policy argument. For doctoral readers, Buolamwini models how Black feminist computer science can drive policy change and offers a vivid case study of theory-practice integration.

**Zuboff, S. (2019). The age of surveillance capitalism. PublicAffairs.**

Zuboff names surveillance capitalism as the dominant economic logic of contemporary platforms, defining it as the unilateral claiming of human experience as raw material for behavioral prediction products. The book traces Google's invention of this logic and its diffusion across industries. While critiqued for under-theorizing race and the Global South, Surveillance Capitalism remains foundational vocabulary for AI-equity scholars.

**Couldry, N., & Mejias, U. A. (2019). The costs of connection: How data is colonizing human life and appropriating it for capitalism. Stanford University Press.**

Couldry and Mejias theorize "data colonialism" as a continuation of historical colonialism's logic of appropriation, now extracted from human experience itself. The book centers Global South perspectives and demands that platform critique attend to long histories of dispossession. For doctoral readers in decolonial AI, this is essential reading and indispensable companion to Zuboff.

**Mohamed, S., Png, M.-T., & Isaac, W. (2020). Decolonial AI: Decolonial theory as sociotechnical foresight in artificial intelligence. Philosophy & Technology, 33(4), 659–684.**

Though an article rather than a book, this work has become canonical. Mohamed, Png, and Isaac demonstrate how decolonial theory—drawing on Quijano, Mignolo, Mbembe—can ground critique of AI systems. The article identifies algorithmic coloniality, ghost work, and beta testing on the Global South as key sites for analysis. Essential foundation for decolonial AI scholarship.

**Birhane, A. (2021). Algorithmic injustice: A relational ethics approach. Patterns, 2(2), 100205.**

Birhane develops relational ethics, drawing on African philosophy and feminist STS, to argue that algorithmic harms cannot be fixed by individual-level fairness adjustments. Her work, alongside coauthored papers on data audits and Big Tech capture, has reshaped the AI ethics conversation. For doctoral readers, Birhane's body of work models rigorous methodological intervention from a Global South perspective.

**D'Ignazio, C., & Klein, L. F. (2020). Data feminism. MIT Press.**

D'Ignazio and Klein synthesize feminist theory, critical race studies, and data science into seven principles for ethical data work—examining power, challenging hierarchies, embracing pluralism, and considering context. The book is rich with case studies and pedagogically generous. For doctoral readers, Data Feminism is foundational for any equity-focused methodology course.

**Wajcman, J. (2004). TechnoFeminism. Polity Press.**

Wajcman synthesizes feminist STS to theorize how technology and gender mutually constitute one another. The book traces shifts from liberal feminist arguments about access, through cyberfeminist celebrations of cyborg politics, to material feminist engagements with infrastructure. For AI-equity readers, Wajcman is essential historical grounding for contemporary feminist tech scholarship.

**Plant, S. (1997). Zeros and ones: Digital women and the new technoculture. Doubleday.**

Plant's cyberfeminist manifesto reclaims women's invisibilized labor in the history of computing, from Ada Lovelace to Bletchley Park computers to telephone operators. The book is essential reading for doctoral candidates tracing the gendered history of automation and the discursive construction of the masculine "tech industry."

**Sadowski, J. (2020). Too smart: How digital capitalism is extracting data, controlling our lives, and taking over the world. MIT Press.**

Sadowski critiques "smart" infrastructure—cities, homes, devices—as data-extraction regimes serving capitalist accumulation. The book theorizes "data capitalism" and "smartness mandate" as analytics. For AI-equity readers, Sadowski offers an accessible, polemic-yet-rigorous companion to Zuboff and Couldry.

**Crawford, K. (2021). Atlas of AI: Power, politics, and the planetary costs of artificial intelligence. Yale University Press.**

Crawford reframes AI as a planetary extractive industry, tracing lithium mining, data labor, classification systems, and military applications. The book insists AI is "neither artificial nor intelligent" but rather material, embodied, and political. Essential reading for any AI-equity course.

**Mills, C. W. (1997). The racial contract. Cornell University Press.**

Mills's foundational political philosophy text argues that the social contract tradition has always been a racial contract, with white supremacy as its unspoken term. For AI-equity scholars working on fairness and justice frameworks, Mills exposes how universalist theory often encodes particular—and white—political interests.

**Crenshaw, K. W. (1991). Mapping the margins: Intersectionality, identity politics, and violence against women of color. Stanford Law Review, 43(6), 1241–1299.**

Crenshaw's foundational essay introduces intersectionality as a framework for understanding how legal and political categories fail women of color. For AI-equity scholars, intersectionality is indispensable for thinking about classification harms in automated systems.

**Collins, P. H. (1990). Black feminist thought: Knowledge, consciousness, and the politics of empowerment. Unwin Hyman.**

Collins synthesizes Black feminist intellectual tradition into a sociological epistemology, theorizing how marginalized knowledge production sustains liberation. For doctoral readers working at the AI-equity intersection, Collins's epistemological framework is essential—an indispensable theoretical anchor.

**Hooks, B. (1981). Ain't I a woman: Black women and feminism. South End Press.**

Hooks's foundational text traces how American feminism has marginalized Black women and how American Black liberation movements have marginalized women. The book offers crucial theoretical tools for contemporary debates about who AI ethics is for and whose harms it foregrounds.

**Davis, A. Y. (2003). Are prisons obsolete? Seven Stories Press.**

Davis develops abolitionist argument in clear, accessible prose, drawing on her own activist and scholarly experience. For AI-equity readers, Are Prisons Obsolete? is essential context for any work on carceral technology, predictive policing, and the prison-industrial-AI complex.

**Davis, A. Y. (2016). Freedom is a constant struggle: Ferguson, Palestine, and the foundations of a movement. Haymarket Books.**

**Fanon, F. (1952). Black skin, white masks (R. Philcox, Trans.). Grove Press.**
