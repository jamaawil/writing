I write because I cannot think clearly without it.

This sounds like an exaggeration, and I used to think it was. I believed for a long time that writing was the recording of thinking — that you had a thought, and then you wrote it down. The writing was secondary. The thinking came first.

I no longer believe this. When I sit down to write about something I think I understand, I quickly discover how thin that understanding is. The act of committing words to a page forces a precision that mental rehearsal never demands. You can hold a vague idea in your head indefinitely. You cannot write a vague paragraph without noticing that it is vague.

This is why writing is teaching. When you explain something to someone who doesn't already know it, you cannot hide behind the illusion of comprehension. The gaps show up. The writing forces clarity not because you are choosing your words carefully — though you are — but because the words themselves impose a standard. A sentence either makes sense or it doesn't. A paragraph either follows logically from the previous one or it doesn't. The page does not accept fuzzy thinking the way the mind does.

There is also something about the permanence of writing that changes how I engage with ideas. When I read, I annotate. When I have a thought I want to keep, I write it down. Not because I expect to reference those notes constantly, but because the act of writing consolidates the idea. It goes from floating in short-term memory to something encoded, something real.

I write to figure out what I think. I write to hold myself accountable to precision. I write because the act of doing so makes me a better reader, a more careful observer, a clearer speaker. Writing is not separate from thinking. It is the best technology I have found for doing it.
