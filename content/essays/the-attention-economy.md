The phrase "attention economy" was coined by Herbert Simon in 1971 and elaborated by Michael Goldhaber in the 1990s. Their argument was simple: in a world of abundant information, the scarce resource is human attention. Whoever captures it, holds power.

What Simon and Goldhaber could not fully anticipate was the sophistication of the machinery that would be built to harvest it.

Today's social platforms are not publishing tools that happen to have engagement features. They are engagement engines that happen to carry content. The content is the hook. The engagement loop — the pull-to-refresh, the notification, the infinite scroll — is the product. And what the product delivers to advertisers is not eyeballs, exactly. It is time: predictable, monetized, granular attention measured in seconds.

Most people understand this at some level. We have known for years that we are the product, not the customer. And yet we continue. This is not weakness or stupidity. It is a design problem. The engineers who built these systems had access to decades of behavioral psychology, unlimited A/B testing capacity, and a singular objective function: maximize engagement. They were very good at their jobs.

**The asymmetry is the problem.** You walk in with a vague intention — check if anything important happened — and the system meets you with a million micro-decisions engineered to delay your exit. Each individual moment is low stakes: one more scroll, one more video. Accumulated across a billion users and a decade of refinement, the aggregate effect is a wholesale restructuring of how people spend their conscious hours.

What gets displaced matters. When attention is captured by the feed, it is not captured by the book, the conversation, the long project, the quiet walk. These are not equivalent substitutions. The kind of thinking you do in a scroll session — reactive, associative, stimulus-driven — is different in kind from the thinking you do in a three-hour reading session. The first follows the grain of the technology. The second requires resisting it.

I am not opposed to technology. I am not a luddite, and I have no illusions that any of us will log off forever. But I think the discourse around this issue has been stuck in the wrong register. We talk about "screen time" as if the problem is hours. We talk about "social media addiction" as if the problem is compulsion. These framings locate the problem inside the individual, which is exactly where the industry wants it.

The problem is structural. It is a set of incentives that rewards companies for harvesting attention and bears no cost for what that harvesting displaces. The solution, if there is one, requires redesigning those incentives — not just practicing better personal habits, though those matter too.

In the meantime: notice what you do not do anymore. The long-form reading, the unstructured thinking, the hobbies that don't produce content. Those absences are data. They tell you what the attention economy has already extracted.
