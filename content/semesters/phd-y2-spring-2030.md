### DISS-712 — DISS 712 · Dissertation · Body Chapters & Defense

*DISS pillar · PhD Year 2 · Spring 2030*

The defense semester. Three body chapters and the conclusion drafted (~55,000–75,000 words). Two journal articles extracted from chapters submitted before the defense. Public 2-hour recorded defense in April/May 2030.

- ★ **Helen Sword** — *Air & Light & Time & Space*

`XD` **Atwood, M. (2013). MaddAddam. McClelland & Stewart.**

[AI + Equity + Communication] Atwood closes her trilogy by foregrounding the Crakers, the gene-engineered post-humans designed to inherit the earth, and their tentative communicative relationship with surviving humans. The pillars synthesize in the children's storytelling practices that emerge between species, in the resistance hackers who once worked inside the biotech compounds, and in the question of who owns the genome of a sentient creature. A speculative treatise on cross-pillar futures: who speaks, who owns, who survives.

[Equity + Communication + AI] In a post-collapse Toronto where the wealthy have fled and the inner city is run by a drug lord, Ti-Jeanne navigates her Caribbean grandmother's spiritual practice. Hopkinson binds Afro-Caribbean diasporic communication (Yoruba ritual) to a near-future biotech IP plot involving organ harvesting. The pillars meet at the body. A foundational Afrofuturist debut.
`XD` **Liu, C. (2015). The dark forest (J. Martinsen, Trans.). Tor Books. (Original work published 2008)**

[AI + Communication + Equity] Liu's middle volume develops the "dark forest" theory: in a universe of asymmetrical information, every civilization that broadcasts dies. The pillars synthesize in the communication-politics of intergalactic visibility, the inequity of who can hide, and the AI strategist sent forward in time. A canonical cross-pillar science-fiction.

Illuminates Communication/Equity: Atwood's dystopia is unexpectedly a book about the abolition of literacy as a political project. Placed beside platform-policy discourse on content moderation, The Handmaid's Tale illuminates how the right to read and write is the substrate of every other right.

**Braided extracurricular reading**

#### Fiction
`XD` **Egan, J. (2010). A visit from the goon squad. Alfred A. Knopf.**

[Communication + IP + AI] A novel-in-stories about the music industry's collapse from analog A&R culture to a future of "pointer" marketing through children's purchase desires. Egan binds the IP economy of music to the communicative form of the PowerPoint chapter and a near-future of pure-tone music marketed to toddlers via parental devices. The pillars meet in the slow conversion of art into data and data into market. A formal experiment that argues authorship, technology, and inequity (between artist and platform) are inseparable.
`XD` **Hopkinson, N. (2003). The salt roads. Warner Books.**

[Equity + Communication] Three Black women across centuries—a Haitian slave, Charles Baudelaire's Black lover, and Saint Mary of Egypt—are linked by a goddess. Hopkinson binds historical archive politics to the communication practice of the diasporic body. The pillars synthesize through the figure of authorship as channel and ancestor. A novel that argues equity work and communication work are inseparable.
`XD` **Murata, S. (2016). Convenience store woman (G. Tapley Takemori, Trans.). Grove Press. (Original work published 2016)**

[Equity + Communication + AI] A middle-aged Tokyo woman finds peace in the predictable script of convenience-store work. Murata binds the soft AI of algorithmic retail management to the gendered, neurodivergent inequity of being read as broken outside that script. The pillars meet in the manual itself: a communication technology that the protagonist embodies more completely than her colleagues. A short, perfect cross-pillar novel.
`XP` **Atwood, M. (2003). Oryx and Crake. McClelland & Stewart.**

Illuminates AI/IP: Atwood's near-future novel of biotech-driven extinction includes the most prescient depiction in fiction of training-data ethics: the "Extinctathon" game, the pharma-engineered post-humans, the boy genius Crake. Placed beside debates on AI safety, Oryx and Crake illuminates how engineered intelligence is always also engineered intent.

`XD` **Watters, A. (2021). Teaching machines: The history of personalized learning. MIT Press.**

[Equity + AI + Communication] Watters traces the century-long history of "teaching machines" from Pressey and Skinner through programmed instruction to contemporary adaptive-learning platforms. The argument: ed-tech's claims of personalization recapitulate behaviorist assumptions about learning that have been rejected in psychology for decades, yet persist in machine architectures. Methodologically, the book is intellectual history with a critical edge. For a doctoral candidate, Teaching Machines exemplifies how to read contemporary AI systems through the longer arc of behaviorist epistemology, with implications for assessment, surveillance, and the political economy of educational technology.
