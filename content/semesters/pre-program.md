### PRE.A — PRE.A · Reading the Law: Legal Reasoning & Case Briefing

*PRE pillar · Pre-program*

How to read a US judicial opinion, distinguish holding from dicta, brief a case in IRAC, and follow citation chains. The skill that unlocks all subsequent IP coursework.

**Essential books**

- ★ **Ruggero Aldisert** — *Logic for Lawyers*
- ★ **Frederick Schauer** — *Thinking Like a Lawyer*
- ★ **Steven Burton** — *Intro to Law and Legal Reasoning*
- ★ **Hart & Sacks** — *The Legal Process*
- ★ **Merges, Menell & Lemley** — *IP in the New Tech Age\* (intro chapters)*

**Supplementary books**

- ☆ **Bryan Garner** — *The Redbook*
- ☆ **Reference work** — *The Bluebook: A Uniform System of Citation*
- ☆ **Herma Hill Kay** — *Intro to the American Legal System*
- ☆ **John Makdisi** — *Intro to the Study of Law*
- ☆ **Kent Syverud** — *The Rules of the Game*

**Papers (48)**

**1. Frederick Schauer (1988).** *Formalism* Late 20th century — legal theory consolidation
Yale Law Journal, 97(4), 509–548

> Argues that rule-based formalism is a defensible and often functional approach to legal decision-making, not merely a naive failure of judgment; distinguishes strong from weak formalism and rehabilitates the concept against realist critique.

**2. Cass R. Sunstein & Adrian Vermeule (2002).** *Interpretation and Institutions* Early 21st century — institutional turn
Michigan Law Review, 101(4), 885–951

> Proposes that legal interpretation cannot be evaluated apart from the institutional actors doing the interpreting; calls for "institutional turn" that considers judicial competence and systemic effects rather than abstract interpretive theories alone.

**3. Richard A. Posner (2005).** *The Law and Economics of Contract Interpretation* Early 21st century — law and economics maturity
Texas Law Review, 83(6), 1581–1614

> Applies economic reasoning to the problem of contractual ambiguity, showing how wealth-maximization norms can systematically guide judicial interpretive choices — an accessible model of law-and-economics legal reasoning for entering students.

**4. Richard A. Posner (1990).** *What Has Pragmatism to Offer Law?* Late 20th century — pragmatist jurisprudence
Southern California Law Review, 63(6), 1653–1670

> Argues that philosophical pragmatism — judging ideas by their practical consequences rather than correspondence to fixed principles — is the most honest description of how good judges actually reason; foundational for realist legal reasoning.

**5. Philip Bobbitt (1991).** *Reflections Inspired by My Critics* Late 20th century — constitutional theory
Texas Law Review, 72(7), 1869–1920

> Responds to critics of his "modalities of constitutional argument" framework; defends the plurality of legitimate legal arguments (textual, historical, prudential, structural, doctrinal, ethical) as a positive account of legal reasoning, not relativism.

**6. Ronald Dworkin (1967).** *The Model of Rules* Mid-20th century — jurisprudential foundations
University of Chicago Law Review, 35(1), 14–46

> Distinguishes legal rules (all-or-nothing application) from principles (dimension of weight), attacking Hart's positivism and arguing that judges draw on principles not reducible to an authoritative rule of recognition; sets the terms of legal theory debate for decades.

**7. H.L.A. Hart (1958).** *Positivism and the Separation of Law and Morals* Mid-20th century — analytic jurisprudence
Harvard Law Review, 71(4), 593–629

> Classic articulation of legal positivism; argues that the question of what the law is and the question of what the law ought to be are analytically separate — entry-level orientation to jurisprudential debate that underpins all legal reasoning instruction.

**8. Karl N. Llewellyn (1930).** *A Realistic Jurisprudence — The Next Step* Early 20th century — legal realism
Columbia Law Review, 30(4), 431–465

> Articulates the realist program for studying law as it is rather than as doctrine declares it; situates the case method within a framework of behavior-prediction, foundational for understanding why students are taught to brief cases rather than memorize rules.

**9. Karl N. Llewellyn (1960).** *The Threat of the Common Law Mind (excerpt from The Common Law Tradition)* Mid-20th century — common law theory
The Common Law Tradition: Deciding Appeals, Little Brown (chapter excerpt reprinted in various casebooks)

> Identifies the "period styles" of common law judging — Grand Style vs. Formal Style — and explains how reading cases against their period style reveals the real ratio; directly trains students in opinion-reading technique.

**10. Henry M. Hart Jr. & Albert M. Sacks (1958 (dist. 1994)).** *The Legal Process: Basic Problems in the Making and Application of Law (Introduction — "The Invitation to Study")* Mid-20th century — legal process school
Foundation Press (Eskridge & Frickey eds., 1994); originally distributed as teaching materials

> Introduces the "institutional competence" framework for allocating legal decisions among courts, legislatures, and agencies; teaches students to think procedurally about which institution decides what and why — grounding for IRAC in systemic context.

**11. Frederick Schauer (1991).** *Playing by the Rules: A Philosophical Examination of Rule-Based Decision-Making in Law and Life (Introduction)* Late 20th century — rule-based legal theory
Oxford University Press (book intro, widely excerpted)

> Frames rule-following as an independent epistemic activity — applying a generalization even when its rationale would not support it in a particular case; the core conceptual move behind IRAC's insistence on stating a rule before analyzing facts.

**12. Steven J. Burton (1985).** *Breach of Contract and the Common Law Duty to Perform in Good Faith* Late 20th century — contract law / legal method
Harvard Law Review, 94(2), 369–404

> Models rigorous IRAC analysis in a doctrinal context (good-faith performance); useful pedagogically as a worked example of how to move from rule statement through fact-rule application to conclusion without leaping past ambiguous steps.

**13. Edward H. Levi (1948).** *An Introduction to Legal Reasoning* Mid-20th century — legal process / analogy
University of Chicago Law Review, 15(3), 501–574

> Describes the three-step common law reasoning pattern — example → rule → next example — showing how legal categories move and shift as new cases are decided; the foundational text for teaching legal analogical reasoning as a distinct cognitive skill.

**14. Richard A. Posner (2008).** *How Judges Think (excerpt: "The Judge as Occasional Legislator")* Early 21st century — judicial behavior
Harvard University Press (chapter excerpt, widely assigned)

> Argues that judges exercise quasi-legislative discretion in hard cases, driven by pragmatic rather than strictly logical considerations; teaches students to read opinions as outputs of human decision-making rather than purely logical deductions.

**15. Harry T. Edwards & Michael A. Livermore (2009).** *Pitfalls of Empirical Studies that Attempt to Understand the Factors Affecting Appellate Decisionmaking* Early 21st century — empirical legal studies
Duke Law Journal, 58(8), 1895–1989

> Critiques empirical legal studies methodology for mischaracterizing judicial opinions; helps students understand the gap between what opinions say and what they measure, and why careful textual reading of opinions remains indispensable.

**16. Lawrence B. Solum (2010).** *The Interpretation-Construction Distinction* Early 21st century — constitutional theory
Constitutional Commentary, 27(1), 95–118

> Distinguishes interpretation (determining linguistic meaning) from construction (determining legal effect) in constitutional adjudication; equips students reading opinions to identify which activity a court is actually performing at any given moment.

**17. Barry Friedman (2006).** *The Politics of Judicial Review* Early 21st century — constitutional history
Texas Law Review, 84(2), 257–337

> Examines how political context shapes judicial review over time; teaches students to situate an opinion in its historical moment, recognizing how extra-doctrinal forces influence what courts say and how they say it.

**18. Ruggero J. Aldisert (1997).** *Logic for Lawyers: A Guide to Clear Legal Thinking (excerpt: "Deduction and Induction in Legal Argument")* Late 20th century — legal logic pedagogy
National Institute for Trial Advocacy (3rd ed., chapter excerpt)

> Breaks judicial syllogisms into their major premise (rule), minor premise (facts), and conclusion, with worked examples from real opinions; essential practical companion for students first learning to parse the logical structure of a court's holding.

**19. Frederick Schauer (1987).** *Precedent* Late 20th century — legal theory
Stanford Law Review, 39(3), 571–605

> REQUIRED TEXT. Offers the most rigorous analytical account of why precedent binds at all; distinguishes the "fact" of precedential constraint from its normative justification, covering formal precedent, gravitational force, and the difference between following and distinguishing. **20. Thomas R. Lee (1999).** *Stare Decisis in Historical Perspective: From the Founding Era to the Rehnquist Court* Late 20th century — legal history
Vanderbilt Law Review, 52(3), 647–735

> Traces the historical development of the stare decisis doctrine in American courts, showing that its current strong form is more recent than commonly assumed; equips students to periodize case law and understand when departures from precedent are plausible.

**21. Evan H. Caminker (1994).** *Why Must Inferior Courts Obey Superior Court Precedents?* Late 20th century — judicial hierarchy
Stanford Law Review, 46(4), 817–873

> Provides the institutional-competence justification for vertical stare decisis; distinguishes hierarchy-based from epistemic rationales for following higher court holdings — directly applicable to briefing cases in a federal system.

**22. David A. Strauss (1996).** *Common Law Constitutional Interpretation* Late 20th century — constitutional common law
University of Chicago Law Review, 63(3), 877–935

> Argues that constitutional law is best understood as an evolving common law tradition rather than as interpretation of a fixed text; explains why precedent plays a larger role than originalist theory admits and how to read constitutional opinions accordingly.

**23. Amy Coney Barrett (1999).** *Stare Decisis and Due Process* Late 20th century — constitutional doctrine
University of Colorado Law Review, 74(4), 1011–1066

> Examines constitutional due-process limits on overruling precedent in reliance-heavy contexts; useful case study in how one brief-worthy doctrine (stare decisis) intersects another (due process), modeling interdisciplinary case analysis for students.

**24. Antonin Scalia (1997).** *Common-Law Courts in a Civil-Law System: The Role of United States Federal Courts in Interpreting the Constitution and Laws* Late 20th century — textualism
In A Matter of Interpretation: Federal Courts and the Law, Princeton University Press, 3–47

> REQUIRED TEXT. Justice Scalia's definitive statement of textualism: federal courts should interpret statutes by their plain meaning rather than legislative history or legislative intent; frames the central interpretive debate students will encounter across all statutory cases.

**25. William N. Eskridge Jr. (1987).** *Dynamic Statutory Interpretation* Late 20th century — dynamic interpretation
University of Pennsylvania Law Review, 135(6), 1479–1555

> Argues that statutory interpretation is and should be dynamic — incorporating evolving circumstances, purposes, and legal contexts rather than being frozen at enactment; the leading counterpoint to Scalia's textualism and essential for balanced case-reading.

**26. John F. Manning (2001).** *Textualism and the Equity of the Statute* Early 21st century — textualism nuance
Columbia Law Review, 101(1), 1–127

> Examines the relationship between textualism and the historical equitable-interpretation tradition; argues textualism is consistent with purposive concerns when text is the exclusive vehicle — important nuance for students taught only the Scalia-Eskridge binary.

**27. Adrian Vermeule (2006).** *Judging Under Uncertainty: An Institutional Theory of Legal Interpretation (excerpt: "The Legislative Process and Its Pathologies")* Early 21st century — institutional theory
Harvard University Press (book chapter, widely excerpted)

> Introduces students to the pathologies of legislative drafting — cycling majorities, agenda manipulation, strategic ambiguity — that explain why statutory text is often unclear and why interpretive choice is unavoidable regardless of one's theory.

**28. Frank H. Easterbrook (1983).** *Statutes' Domains* Late 20th century — statutory interpretation
University of Chicago Law Review, 50(2), 533–552

> Argues that courts should not extend statutes beyond their domain of enacted application; models a narrow, clause-bound reading strategy students can apply when briefing cases involving statutory ambiguity at the margins of a statute's text.

**29. Cass R. Sunstein (1990).** *Interpreting Statutes in the Regulatory State* Late 20th century — administrative law / interpretation
Harvard Law Review, 103(2), 405–508

> Provides a comprehensive taxonomy of canons of statutory construction, situating them in the modern administrative state; introduces students to the interpretive toolkit courts deploy and helps them recognize which canon a given opinion is applying.

**30. Philip Bobbitt (1982).** *Constitutional Fate: Theory of the Constitution (excerpt: "The Modalities of Constitutional Argument")* Late 20th century — constitutional theory
Oxford University Press (book chapter, widely excerpted)

> Identifies six legitimate modalities of constitutional argument (textual, historical, prudential, structural, doctrinal, ethical) and argues that no single modality has priority; gives students a diagnostic map for parsing how any constitutional opinion constructs its argument.

**31. David A. Strauss (2010).** *The Living Constitution (excerpt: "Why the Written Constitution Does Not Matter Much")* Early 21st century — constitutional theory
Oxford University Press (book chapter, widely excerpted)

> Makes the affirmative case that the Constitution's meaning evolves through common-law style development of precedents rather than through text or original intent; complements the Bobbitt structural approach with a historical-developmental argument students should be able to critically evaluate.

**32. Jack M. Balkin (2007).** *Original Meaning and Constitutional Redemption* Early 21st century — originalism / living constitutionalism
Constitutional Commentary, 24(2), 427–532

> Proposes "living originalism" — fidelity to the original meaning of constitutional text combined with evolving construction of application — bridging the originalism vs. living-constitutionalism debate; teaches students to identify hybrid arguments in opinions.

**33. Cass R. Sunstein (1999).** *One Case at a Time: Judicial Minimalism on the Supreme Court (excerpt: "The Case for Minimalism")* Late 20th century — judicial minimalism
Harvard University Press (book chapter, widely excerpted)

> Argues that narrow, shallow, incompletely theorized decisions are often judicially superior to ambitious rulings; teaches students to recognize when a court's ratio is intentionally cabined and to distinguish holding from dicta in minimalist opinions.

**34. Akhil Reed Amar (1998).** *Intratextualism* Late 20th century — constitutional text
Harvard Law Review, 112(4), 747–827

> Develops the technique of reading constitutional provisions in light of other constitutional provisions rather than in isolation or through external history; a concrete, teachable method of textual interpretation students can apply to any constitutional opinion.

**35. Bryan A. Garner (2002).** *The Redbook: A Manual on Legal Style (excerpt: "Editing for Concision and Precision")* Early 21st century — legal writing practice
West Academic (3rd ed., chapter excerpt)

> Provides the stylistic baseline for legal writing in American courts; directly applicable to the case brief assignment format in IRAC structure, with concrete guidance on sentence-level precision that differentiates competent from strong legal prose.

**36. Eugene Volokh (2010).** *Academic Legal Writing: Law Review Articles, Student Notes, Seminar Papers, and Getting on Law Review (excerpt: "Making Your Article Clear a* Early 21st century — academic legal writing
Foundation Press (4th ed., chapter excerpt)

> Translates academic legal writing norms into learnable techniques; relevant to pre-program students because the habits of precise, well-organized legal writing transfer directly to the case brief format and IRAC structure used throughout the program.

**37. Martin A. Eppler (2006).** *A Comparison of Common Methods for Representing Arguments in Legal Reasoning* Early 21st century — legal pedagogy / AI & law
Artificial Intelligence and Law, 14(4), 295–323

> Compares structured argument-mapping formats (IRAC, issue trees, argument diagrams) and empirically assesses which aids comprehension; directly informs how students should construct and evaluate case briefs as a cognitive tool, not merely a formatting exercise.

**38. Linda H. Edwards (2007).** *The Convergence of Analogical and Dialectical Imagining in Legal Discourse* Early 21st century — legal writing pedagogy
Legal Writing: The Journal of the Legal Writing Institute, 9(1), 1–28

> Examines how legal writers cognitively move between analogy (case comparison) and dialectic (rule application) when composing briefs and memos; offers a process model for the IRAC move from rule to analysis that is more sophisticated than the mechanical formula.

**39. Ruth Anne Robbins (2006).** *An Introduction to Visual Legal Rhetoric* Early 21st century — visual legal communication
Legal Communication & Rhetoric: JALWD, 3(1), 141–165

> Introduces visual design principles as components of persuasive legal communication; useful for students to understand that the visual organization of a case brief (headings, white space, paragraph discipline) is itself rhetorical and affects how judges process arguments.

**40. Peter Goodrich (1987).** *Legal Discourse: Studies in Linguistics, Rhetoric and Legal Analysis (excerpt: "The Rhetoric of Judgment")* Late 20th century — legal rhetoric
Macmillan (book chapter, widely excerpted)

> Analyzes the rhetorical structures of judicial discourse — how conventions of argumentation, address, and authority construct the persuasive effect of an opinion independent of its logical content; trains students to read beyond the syllogistic surface of any opinion.

**41. James Boyd White (1985).** *Law as Rhetoric, Rhetoric as Law: The Arts of Cultural and Communal Life* Late 20th century — law and humanities
University of Chicago Law Review, 52(3), 684–702

> Argues that law is a form of literary and rhetorical practice, not only a logical system; treating opinions as acts of persuasion and community-constituting discourse equips students to read them with the interpretive sophistication of humanistic close reading.

**Braided extracurricular reading**

#### Fiction
`COMM` **DeLillo, D. (1985). White noise. Viking Press.**

DeLillo's airborne toxic event is also an airborne mediated event: television, supermarket tabloids, radio chatter, and academic discourse on Hitler studies converge to constitute reality itself. The Gladney family lives inside what Baudrillard would call hyperreality, where "the most photographed barn in America" exists primarily as photograph. The novel anticipates platform-era questions about secondary virality and the disappearance of unmediated experience. Its method is satiric ethnography of a media-saturated middle class. For a doctoral candidate at the COMM/IP/AI/equity nexus, White Noise models how communication infrastructure shapes ontology, foreshadowing debates about generative AI's epistemic effects and the political economy of attention.
`COMM` **Egan, J. (2010). A visit from the goon squad. Alfred A. Knopf.**

Egan's Pulitzer-winning linked-story novel traces music industry figures across decades, ending in a near-future of "pointers," child-driven music consumption, and ambient social media. One chapter is rendered entirely as PowerPoint slides. Its method is formal experimentation across genres of mediation. Goon Squad dramatizes how the music industry's transformation by digital platforms preceded and prefigured broader media disruption. Essential for COMM/IP doctoral candidates studying copyright in the streaming era, the collapse of traditional gatekeepers, and the affective texture of platform-mediated cultural production. Pairs well with Sterne, Cory Doctorow, and Burkart on music and IP.
`COMM` **Zamyatin, Y. (1924). We (G. Zilboorg, Trans.). E. P. Dutton.**

Zamyatin's foundational dystopia, written in 1920–21, depicts the One State where citizens are numbers, glass apartments enforce transparency, and a state-sanctioned newspaper controls reality. The narrator's diary becomes the novel's text. Its method is mathematical-modernist anti-utopia. We preceded Orwell and Huxley and remains essential reading for COMM scholarship on totalitarian communication, surveillance architecture, and the literary form of dystopian critique. For doctoral candidates working at the COMM/equity intersection, Zamyatin's depiction of transparency-as-control speaks directly to contemporary platform ideology that conflates visibility with virtue, themes Eggers and Zuboff later interrogate.
`COMM` **Achebe, C. (1958). Things fall apart. William Heinemann.**

Achebe's foundational novel of colonial encounter in Igbo society depicts oral traditions, proverbs, ritual communication, and the disruptive arrival of missionary writing. Its method is realist novel deeply infused with Igbo communicative forms. Things Fall Apart is essential reading for COMM/equity doctoral candidates on orality/literacy debates (think Walter Ong, Eric Havelock), colonial epistemic violence, and the politics of which communicative forms get archived. Achebe's careful representation of Igbo proverbial speech anticipates contemporary debates about whose linguistic practices get encoded in AI training data and whose remain epistemically marginal.
`COMM` **Lahiri, J. (2003). The namesake. Houghton Mifflin.**

Lahiri's novel of the Ganguli family follows Gogol Ganguli, an Indian American whose name itself becomes a site of cross-cultural communicative struggle. Letters, photographs, and trans-Atlantic phone calls figure throughout. Its method is restrained realist family fiction. The Namesake is essential for COMM/equity doctoral work on diasporic communication, generational mediation, and the politics of naming. Lahiri models how diaspora communities maintain communicative connection across distance and time, a topic now central to platform-era studies of transnational digital practice (e.g., Madianou and Miller's Migration and New Media).
#### Nonfiction
`COMM` **Innis, H. A. (1951). The bias of communication. University of Toronto Press.**

Innis's foundational essays argue that every communication medium possesses a "bias" toward either time (durable, religious, communal) or space (portable, secular, imperial), and that civilizations rise and fall with these biases. His method is comparative historical political economy of media. The Bias of Communication establishes communication infrastructure as constitutive of political and economic possibility, prefiguring the work of his student McLuhan, the Toronto School, and contemporary platform studies (Plantin, Lampland, Star, Edwards). For doctoral candidates at the COMM/IP/AI/equity intersection, Innis provides the foundational lens for analyzing how AI infrastructures (data centers, undersea cables, chip fabs) constitute geopolitical and epistemological biases.
`COMM` **Tufekci, Z. (2017). Twitter and tear gas: The power and fragility of networked protest. Yale University Press.**

Tufekci's foundational study draws on her embedded research with movements from Tahrir Square to Occupy, Black Lives Matter, and beyond, analyzing how social media simultaneously enable rapid mobilization and undermine organizational capacity. Her method is participant-observation embedded research combined with structural analysis. Twitter and Tear Gas is essential reading for COMM/equity doctoral work on social movements and digital media. For doctoral candidates working on platform politics, Tufekci provides the most rigorous empirical account of how platform affordances shape mobilization and what movements gain and lose from them.
`COMM` **Florini, S. (2019). Beyond hashtags: Racial politics and Black digital networks. NYU Press.**

Florini's foundational study analyzes Black podcast networks and Twitter publics as sites of racial politics that exceed the hashtag-driven media coverage of Black Twitter. Her method is ethnographic digital cultural studies. Beyond Hashtags is essential reading for COMM/equity doctoral work on Black digital practice. For doctoral candidates, Florini offers a methodological model for studying minoritized communities' communicative practice across multiple platforms, complicating single-platform analysis.
`COMM` **Solove, D. J. (2008). Understanding privacy. Harvard University Press.**

Solove's foundational legal-theoretical study develops a taxonomy of privacy harms to overcome the "I have nothing to hide" objection. His method is legal theory in dialogue with technology studies. Understanding Privacy is essential reading for COMM/IP doctoral work. For doctoral candidates working at the platform/AI intersection, Solove's taxonomy provides the conceptual vocabulary for distinguishing surveillance from aggregation from secondary use, essential for sophisticated privacy analysis.
`COMM` **Jenkins, H., Ito, M., & boyd, d. (2015). Participatory culture in a networked era. Polity.**

Jenkins, Ito, and boyd's dialogic study revisits participatory culture in light of platform-era developments. Their method is multi-author dialogue grounded in their respective research traditions. Participatory Culture in a Networked Era is essential reading for COMM doctoral work. For doctoral candidates, the volume models how foundational scholars revise earlier frameworks in light of platform consolidation, content moderation, and inequality concerns.

`COMM` **DeLillo, D. (1991). Mao II. Viking Press.**

"The future belongs to crowds," declares Bill Gray, the reclusive novelist whose photograph becomes more potent than his prose. DeLillo stages a confrontation between the solitary author and the terrorist as rival communicators competing for narrative authority in an image-saturated age. The novel's meditation on photography, celebrity, mass gatherings, and hostage media remains startlingly prescient about virality, deepfakes, and platform attention economies. Its method is dialogic, juxtaposing the writer's interiority with crowd phenomenology. Essential for COMM scholars examining how mass media reorganizes the public sphere and erodes authorial control, with direct bearing on contemporary IP debates over image rights and AI-generated likenesses.
`COMM` **Egan, J. (2022). The candy house. Scribner.**

Egan's sequel imagines "Own Your Unconscious," a technology allowing users to upload their memories to a collective consciousness. The novel explores authenticity, surveillance, and what remains private when memory becomes shareable data. Its method is interlinked narrative across multiple perspectives, registers, and chronologies. The Candy House is essential for COMM/AI/IP doctoral work, dramatizing the political economy and intimate phenomenology of biometric/cognitive data extraction. Egan's novel resonates with Zuboff's surveillance capitalism critique and anticipates regulatory debates about neural data, biometric IP, and consent in AI training. It models speculative fiction as theory-building.

Murakami's epic alternative-reality novel features twin protagonists who slip into a parallel "1Q84," where a cult publishes a strange novel called Air Chrysalis that becomes a media sensation. Its method is dual-narrator magical realism. 1Q84 is essential for COMM/IP doctoral work on authorship, ghostwriting, and viral media phenomena. Murakami's exploration of how a single book reshapes consensus reality anticipates concerns about platform-driven epistemic crisis and viral disinformation.

Coetzee's exploration of Lurie's refusal of public penitence raises questions about platform-era apology culture, the rhetoric of accountability, and racialized economies of forgiveness.

`COMM` **McLuhan, M. (1964). Understanding media: The extensions of man. McGraw-Hill.**

McLuhan's foundational claim that "the medium is the message" inaugurates the analytic that media's formal properties matter more than their content. The book taxonomizes media into "hot" and "cool" categories and tracks their psychic and social effects. Its method is provocative aphoristic media archaeology. Understanding Media remains essential reading for COMM doctoral work despite (and partly because of) its eccentricities. For doctoral candidates at the COMM/IP/AI/equity intersection, Noble provides the foundational analysis of how search-engine intellectual property structures (proprietary algorithms, opaque ranking) compound racial harm.

Their method is collaborative digital ethnography. Hanging Out, Messing Around, and Geeking Out is essential reading for COMM doctoral work on youth digital practice. For doctoral candidates, Ito et al. provide the foundational empirical study of youth digital participation.

`COMM` **DeLillo, D. (1988). Libra. Viking Press.**
