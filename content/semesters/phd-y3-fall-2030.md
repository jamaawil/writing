**Braided extracurricular reading**

#### Fiction
`XD` **Egan, J. (2022). The candy house. Scribner.**

[AI + Communication + Equity] Egan's loose sequel imagines "Own Your Unconscious," a tech that allows users to upload their memories to a collective archive. The pillars synthesize through privacy refuseniks ("eluders"), the communication-platform politics of memory ownership, and the inequities of who can afford anonymity. The novel braids consent, IP-in-memory, and platform sociality. A definitive cross-pillar fiction for the 2020s.
`XD` **Okorafor, N. (2010). Who fears death. DAW.**

[Equity + Communication + AI] In a post-apocalyptic Sudanese future, Onyesonwu, a mixed-race girl conceived in violence, undertakes a journey to confront the wizard who orchestrated her people's genocide. Okorafor binds genocidal communication weapons (the "Great Book"), proto-AI computer remnants, and gendered racial inequity. The pillars meet in the burning archive. A canonical Africanfuturist cross-pillar novel.
`XD` **Tokarczuk, O. (2017). Drive your plow over the bones of the dead (A. Lloyd-Jones, Trans.). Riverhead. (Original work published 2009)**

[Communication + Equity] An aging Polish woman, devoted to astrology and animal rights, narrates a series of deaths in a remote village. Tokarczuk binds the communication politics of marginal speech (the protagonist is dismissed as "crazy") to the equity question of who can be heard. The pillars meet in her astrological dispatches.
`XP` **Whitehead, C. (2011). Zone one. Doubleday.**

Illuminates Equity: Whitehead's literary zombie novel set in post-plague Manhattan is a study in how disaster capitalism reorganizes labor. Placed beside scholarship on platform precarity, Zone One illuminates how cleanup, sorting, and waste work — the lowest-status jobs in any infrastructure — are always racialized and feminized. A cross-pollination for thinking about content moderation as zombie work.
#### Nonfiction
`XD` **O'Neil, C. (2016). Weapons of math destruction: How big data increases inequality and threatens democracy. Crown.**

[AI + Equity + Communication] O'Neil writes from inside finance and tech to describe how "WMDs"—opaque, scaled, damaging algorithms—reproduce inequity in education, credit, employment, and policing. The pillars synthesize in the popular communication of an algorithmic audit. A bestselling cross-pillar text.
`XD` **Nakamura, L. (2007). Digitizing race: Visual cultures of the Internet. University of Minnesota Press.**

[Equity + Communication + AI] Nakamura analyzes the visual semiotics of race online, from avatars to pregnancy boards. The pillars synthesize in the screenshot. A canonical text.
`XD` **Glissant, É. (1997). Poetics of relation (B. Wing, Trans.). University of Michigan Press. (Original work published 1990)**

[Equity + Communication] Glissant's poetics of opacity and relation argues for the right to refuse transparency to colonial communication regimes. The pillars synthesize in the "Right to Opacity" chapter. A foundational text for cross-pillar communication ethics.
`XP` **Boltanski, L., & Chiapello, E. (2005). The new spirit of capitalism (G. Elliott, Trans.). Verso. (Original work published in French)**

Illuminates Equity: Boltanski and Chiapello's sociology of post-1968 management discourse is a cross-pollination text for platform-management critique.

#### Fiction
`XD` **Powers, R. (2018). The overstory. W. W. Norton.**

[Communication + Equity + AI] Powers braids nine human lives with the longer life of trees, and a key strand features a programmer building Mastery, a series of life-simulation games that mirrors the data-driven flattening of ecological intelligence. The pillars synthesize in how communication networks—digital and arboreal—are co-opted by extraction. The novel argues that AI and forests share a problem: human inability to listen to non-human intelligence in conditions of inequity.
`XD` **Okorafor, N. (2015). The book of Phoenix. DAW.**

[AI + Equity + IP] A genetically engineered "accelerated" woman named Phoenix wakes inside Tower 7, a corporation experimenting on Black bodies, and burns her way to freedom. Okorafor binds biotech IP (Phoenix is corporate property), AI surveillance, and the equity politics of human-experimentation. The pillars meet in Phoenix's manuscript-as-bomb. A prequel to Who Fears Death that sharpens the cross-pillar argument.
`XD` **Adichie, C. N. (2025). Dream count. Alfred A. Knopf.**

[Equity + Communication] Adichie's first novel in over a decade braids four Black women across continents and includes a stark, sustained account of a hotel-housekeeper's accusation of a major political figure (modeled on the Dominique Strauss-Kahn case). The pillars synthesize through the communication politics of who is believed and the inequity of legal procedure across borders. The novel's polyphonic structure is itself an argument about whose voice counts.
`XP` **Egan, J. (2010). A visit from the goon squad. Knopf.**

Illuminates Communication/IP: Egan's novel of music-industry characters, including a chapter rendered as PowerPoint slides, is a study in formal experiment with platform-shaped attention. Placed beside platform-studies scholarship, Goon Squad illuminates how literary form responds to media form. The Sasha-Lincoln chapters anticipate Twitter and TikTok narrative grammar by years.
#### Nonfiction
`XD` **Mayer-Schönberger, V., & Cukier, K. (2013). Big data: A revolution that will transform how we live, work, and think. Houghton Mifflin Harcourt.**

[AI + Communication + Equity] Although celebratory in tone, this early popular text articulates the cross-pillar argument that bulk data analysis would reshape law, policy, and inequity at scale. The pillars synthesize in the chapter on datafication of human movement. A pre-2018 reference for the field.
`XD` **Nakamura, L. (2002). Cybertypes: Race, ethnicity, and identity on the Internet. Routledge.**

[Equity + Communication] Nakamura's earlier work coins "cybertype" as the racialized stereotype that internet visual culture reproduces. The pillars synthesize in the menu of "racial" options on character-creation screens.
`XD` **Spivak, G. C. (1988). Can the subaltern speak? In C. Nelson & L. Grossberg (Eds.), Marxism and the interpretation of culture (pp. 271–313). University of Illinois Press.**

[Equity + Communication] Spivak's question—Can the subaltern speak?—joins postcolonial theory and communication theory. The pillars synthesize in the figure of the woman whose suicide cannot be heard. Foundational.
`XP` **Lefebvre, H. (1991). The production of space (D. Nicholson-Smith, Trans.). Blackwell. (Original work published in French)**

Illuminates Communication/AI: Lefebvre's foundational text on spatial production is a cross-pollination with platform-architecture scholarship.
