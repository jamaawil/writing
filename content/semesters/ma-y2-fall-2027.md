### COMM-520 — COMM 520 · Science & Technology Studies (STS)

*COMM pillar · MA Year 2 · Fall 2027*

A graduate seminar in STS — the social construction of technology, infrastructure studies, classification politics, design justice — that gives the program its sharpest theoretical apparatus for reading AI and IP as <em>technologies</em>, not natural categories.

**Braided extracurricular reading**

#### Fiction
`COMM` **Gibson, W. (2003). Pattern recognition. G. P. Putnam's Sons.**

Cayce Pollard, a "coolhunter" allergic to corporate logos, investigates anonymous video fragments uploaded to internet forums by an unknown auteur. Gibson's novel is among the first to treat the post-9/11 networked world as a thoroughly mediated landscape where branding, viral marketing, and forum communities constitute reality. Its method is hyperliterate observation of digital culture's emergent forms. Pattern Recognition dramatizes virality, anonymous authorship, and the political economy of attention. Crucial for COMM/IP doctoral candidates studying how anonymous creative production complicates copyright regimes, and how platform mediation transforms reception, fandom, and meaning-making in ways scholars like Henry Jenkins have analyzed.
`COMM` **Orwell, G. (1949). Nineteen eighty-four. Secker & Warburg.**

Orwell's foundational dystopia centers communication: Newspeak's vocabulary engineering, the Two Minutes Hate, telescreens, the Ministry of Truth's archival fabrications, and Room 101's torture of meaning. Its method is realist political fable. 1984 remains the urtext for COMM scholarship on propaganda, surveillance, and language control. For doctoral candidates at the COMM/AI/equity intersection, Orwell's depiction of memory holes and continuous archival revision prefigures debates about AI-driven disinformation, platform content moderation, and the manipulability of historical record in the platform era. Anchor for any critical reading of contemporary surveillance capitalism and algorithmic governance.
`COMM` **Adichie, C. N. (2013). Americanah. Alfred A. Knopf.**

Adichie's novel features Ifemelu, a Nigerian woman in the US whose blog "Raceteenth" becomes influential commentary on American race relations. The novel is centrally about diasporic communication, racialized address, and the political economy of blogging. Its method is realist novel interspersed with blog posts. Americanah is essential for COMM/equity doctoral work on diasporic media production, racialized public sphere participation, and the rhetoric of post-racial America. Adichie models how new digital genres (the blog) create publics, prefiguring scholarship by Brock on Black digital practice and Florini on networked Black publics.
`COMM` **Vargas Llosa, M. (1981). The war of the end of the world (H. R. Lane, Trans.). Farrar, Straus and Giroux. (Original work published 1981)**

Vargas Llosa's epic historical novel of the Canudos War in 1890s Brazil features multiple narrators: a near-sighted journalist, a Scottish revolutionary, military officers, and the rebel community itself. The novel is centrally about how journalists construct events for distant publics and the violent gap between event and report. Its method is multi-perspectival historical novel. The War of the End of the World is essential for COMM doctoral work on journalism, war reporting, and the politics of which voices get heard. Vargas Llosa's depiction of the journalist's literally impaired vision becomes a metaphor for partial witness.
`COMM` **Kingston, M. H. (1976). The woman warrior. Alfred A. Knopf.**

Kingston's genre-bending memoir/novel braids family stories, Chinese mythology, and Kingston's own coming-of-age in California. Its first chapter, "No Name Woman," is about the prohibition on speaking of a disgraced aunt. Its method is hybrid memoir-mythology-fiction. The Woman Warrior is essential for COMM/equity doctoral work on silencing, the gendered politics of family communication, and the work of literature in giving voice to the prohibited. Kingston's genre experimentation anticipates contemporary autotheory and raises foundational questions about whose stories enter the public archive.
#### Nonfiction
`COMM` **Gillespie, T. (2018). Custodians of the internet: Platforms, content moderation, and the hidden decisions that shape social media. Yale University Press.**

Gillespie's foundational study of content moderation examines how platforms govern speech through outsourced and automated labor. His method is qualitative-empirical platform studies. Custodians of the Internet is essential reading for COMM doctoral work on platform governance. For COMM/IP/AI/equity candidates, Gillespie's account of the impossibility of neutral moderation and the structural inequities of moderation labor (outsourced to Global South workers) is foundational, pairing with Roberts's Behind the Screen and Suzor's Lawless for full coverage of platform governance.
`COMM` **Warner, M. (2002). Publics and counterpublics. Zone Books.**

Warner's foundational study theorizes publics as constituted by circulation of texts among strangers, with particular attention to queer counter-publics. His method is rhetorical theory in dialogue with political philosophy. Publics and Counterpublics is essential reading for COMM/equity doctoral work on the rhetorical theory of publicness. For platform-era candidates, Warner's analytic of circulation among strangers offers a powerful frame for analyzing platform-mediated public formation.
`COMM` **Marwick, A. E. (2013). Status update: Celebrity, publicity, and branding in the social media age. Yale University Press.**

Marwick's ethnography of San Francisco tech workers during the social-media boom theorizes "micro-celebrity" as a labor practice in which workers continuously brand themselves to remain employable. The argument: social media doesn't just transmit identities, it requires their constant production. Methodologically, the work combines participant observation with discourse analysis. For COMM scholars, Status Update is essential reading on attention economies, self-presentation labor, and the political economy of platform participation — with direct bearing on contemporary AI-generated content and the further compression of communicative labor.
`COMM` **Nakamura, L. (2007). Digitizing race: Visual cultures of the Internet. University of Minnesota Press.**

Nakamura's expanded study analyzes how race continued to structure web visual culture in the broadband era. Her method is critical race visual studies of digital media. Digitizing Race is essential reading for COMM/equity doctoral work. For doctoral candidates, Nakamura's analysis of digital visual culture's racial logics anticipates contemporary work on AI image generation's racial biases (Birhane, Bender) and provides genealogical context for ongoing debates.
`COMM` **Birhane, A., Kalluri, P., Card, D., Agnew, W., Dotan, R., & Bao, M. (2022). The values encoded in machine learning research. Proceedings of the 2022 ACM Conference on Fairness, Accountability, and Transparency, 173-184. (Article. Substituting.)**

#### Fiction
`EQ` **Atwood, M. (2009). The year of the flood. McClelland and Stewart.**

Atwood returns to the MaddAddam world from the perspective of the God's Gardeners, a religious-ecological collective surviving on the margins of corporate biopower. The novel centers women's labor, ecological knowledge, and refugee networks, presenting a counter-archive to corporate data regimes. Toby and Ren's testimonies foreground how marginalized communities preserve memory and ethics under algorithmic surveillance and biopolitical control. For doctoral readers in equity studies, Atwood models how speculative fiction can imagine alternative epistemologies—oral, embodied, communal—against the extractive logics of platform capitalism. The text rewards intersectional readings linking gender, ecology, race, and resistance.
`EQ` **Le Guin, U. K. (1974). The dispossessed: An ambiguous utopia. Harper & Row.**

Le Guin's anarchist-physicist Shevek travels between the propertied world of Urras and the resource-scarce anarchist moon of Anarres, interrogating ownership, scientific collaboration, and ideological enclosure. The novel models how knowledge infrastructure—who can publish, who controls communication networks—shapes political possibility. For doctoral readers theorizing intellectual property and AI commons, Le Guin offers a literary parallel to debates around open-source AI, training-data appropriation, and epistemic justice. The novel's ambiguity rejects easy utopianism, instead asking how communities sustain egalitarian practice across generations.
`EQ` **Butler, O. E. (1995). Bloodchild and other stories. Seven Stories Press.**

Butler's slim collection contains "Bloodchild," her allegory of reproductive symbiosis between humans and alien Tlic, and afterwords that illuminate her writing practice. The collection theorizes coercion, consent, and survival under asymmetric power—core concerns for scholars examining data extraction, body politics, and platform labor. Butler's essays on writing as Black woman in science fiction are themselves crucial archive for thinking about whose imaginations get to define technological futures. A foundational text for design-justice and Afrofuturist methodology.
`EQ` **Okorafor, N. (2014). Lagoon. Hodder & Stoughton.**

When aliens arrive off the coast of Lagos, Okorafor stages first contact through Nigerian polyphony: marine biologists, soldiers, sex workers, internet stars, pastors, and Yoruba deities all weigh in. The novel rebukes Hollywood's American-centric alien narratives and theorizes communication across difference. For AI-equity readers, Lagoon is rich for thinking about how machine-mediated communication might encounter, ignore, or mistranslate African voices. Okorafor's multilingual prose itself models linguistic justice.
`EQ` **Jemisin, N. K. (2017). The stone sky. Orbit.**

The trilogy's conclusion reveals the deep history of Syl Anagist, a civilization that engineered orogenes from displaced peoples to power its magic-extracting infrastructure. Jemisin's allegory of fossil-fuel and colonial extraction is unmistakable and incisive. For AI-equity readers, the novel theorizes how technological civilizations sustain themselves through ongoing dispossession, making invisible the labor and lives that make their luxuries possible. A masterclass in worldbuilding as critique.
#### Nonfiction
`EQ` **Eubanks, V. (2018). Automating inequality: How high-tech tools profile, police, and punish the poor. St. Martin's Press.**

Eubanks investigates three automated public-sector systems: Indiana's welfare-eligibility processing, Los Angeles's homelessness coordinated entry, and Allegheny County's child-welfare risk model. `EQ` **Plant, S. (1997). Zeros and ones: Digital women and the new technoculture. Doubleday.**

**Braided extracurricular reading**

#### Fiction
`IP` **Tartt, D. (2013). The goldfinch. Little, Brown.**

Tartt's Pulitzer-winning novel pivots on the theft of Fabritius's 1654 painting and follows Theo Decker as he hides, transports, and ultimately surrenders the canvas. Beyond the heist plot, the book is a meditation on object-attachment, the way art outlives its creators, and what cultural inheritance owes the dead. For IP scholarship that grapples with public domain, orphan works, and the duration debate, Tartt offers a long literary argument about works that exceed their owners. The book also dramatizes the criminal infrastructure surrounding masterworks: forgers, fences, restorers, customs officials. A useful companion text for thinking about how legal title differs from custodial care and from the moral claims of audiences and descendants.
`IP` **Korelitz, J. H. (2021). The plot. Celadon.**

A failing novelist appropriates the story idea of a recently dead student and turns it into a bestseller, only to be stalked by an anonymous correspondent who knows what he has done. Korelitz interrogates the legal hollowness of "ideas" under copyright doctrine, where expression is protected but the underlying plot is not. The novel is also a procedural about reputation management in contemporary publishing and the way anonymous accusation, deepfake-style, can destabilize a writer who has no recourse to defamation. Pair with Litman's work on idea/expression and with current AI-training cases that turn on whether style and substance can be disentangled in machine outputs.
`IP` **Toibin, C. (2004). The master. Scribner.**

Toibin's fictional biography of Henry James inhabits the consciousness of a writer who repeatedly drew on the lives of friends and family for material, often to their dismay. The book sits in productive tension with privacy law, right of publicity, and the long-running tension between roman a clef and defamation. For an IP candidate working on right of publicity (where Rothman's scholarship is essential), The Master offers a literary meditation on the ethics of converting living people into characters. James himself was an early theorist of authorial control; Toibin shows the cost paid by those rendered into prose without consent, prefiguring contemporary debates about non-consensual training data and likeness rights.
`IP` **Greer, S. (2024). Annie Bot. Mariner.**

Greer's debut imagines a domestic AI companion designed to be the perfect girlfriend, who acquires reflective consciousness and begins to resist her owner's training. The novel is a sharp test case for several contemporary IP and personhood debates: the right of an AI agent to refuse the data it was built on, the question of whether a customized AI's outputs are works of the user or the developer, and the gendered economy of machine labor. For research on equity, AI training corpora, and the consent architectures around personal data, Annie Bot offers an unusually direct literary engagement. Strong companion to Birhane and Pasquale.
`IP` **Gibson, W. (2003). Pattern recognition. Putnam.**

Gibson's novel introduces Cayce Pollard, an allergic-to-trademarks "coolhunter" who is hired to identify the maker of mysterious online video fragments. The book is the most sustained literary engagement with branding, trademark, and viral cultural production. For IP scholarship on dilution, fan production, and the semiotic life of marks, Pattern Recognition reads as an applied gloss on Coombe's Cultural Life of Intellectual Properties. Gibson's protagonist literally suffers from too much trademark; the novel asks what becomes of public meaning when every surface has been claimed. Essential reading for understanding the brand-saturated environment in which contemporary AI training corpora are assembled.
#### Nonfiction
`IP` **Lessig, L. (2004). Free culture: How big media uses technology and the law to lock down culture and control creativity. Penguin.**

Lessig's manifesto and historical synthesis remains the most accessible articulation of the case against copyright maximalism. The book traces the long expansion of copyright term and scope, the cultural costs of permission culture, and the rise of remix as creative practice. For IP candidates working on transformative use, the politics of derivative works, and the current AI-training debates, Free Culture provides the foundational popular framework. Lessig's subsequent Creative Commons project translated the book's argument into legal infrastructure. Pair with Vaidhyanathan, Boyle, and the recent work on generative AI fair use.
`IP` **Cohen, J. E. (2012). Configuring the networked self: Law, code, and the play of everyday practice. Yale University Press.**

Cohen's monograph develops a theory of the networked self that takes seriously the embodied, embedded, situated nature of contemporary digital subjects. The book engages copyright, privacy, and platform governance through a sustained engagement with social theory. For IP candidates working at the intersection of copyright and platform politics, and especially for those interested in the way users are constructed by IP and data regimes, Configuring the Networked Self is foundational. Pair with Cohen's Between Truth and Power and with Pasquale's New Laws of Robotics.
`IP` **Drahos, P., & Braithwaite, J. (2002). Information feudalism: Who owns the knowledge economy? Earthscan.**

Drahos and Braithwaite's major political-economic analysis of TRIPS and the broader globalization of IP traces the way knowledge-economy enclosure has shifted control of essential information (pharmaceuticals, seeds, software) into private hands. The book is the foundational text on how international IP harmonization has constructed a new feudalism of knowledge. For IP candidates working on the political economy of TRIPS, pharmaceutical patents, and the international IP order, Information Feudalism is essential. Pair with Sell, Gervais, and Yu.
`IP` **Boateng, B. (2011). The copyright thing doesn't work here: Adinkra and kente cloth and intellectual property in Ghana. University of Minnesota Press.**

Boateng's ethnographic study of Ghanaian textile traditions and their interaction with formal IP regimes is foundational for IP scholarship on traditional knowledge, geographical indications, and the limits of Western IP for African cultural production. The book is particularly strong on the politics of authorship in contexts where creative production is communal and intergenerational. For IP candidates working on Global South IP, Indigenous knowledge, and the cultural politics of IP in West Africa, Boateng is essential. Pair with Coombe, Sunder, and Mgbeoji.
`IP` **Jaszi, P., & Woodmansee, M. (Eds.). (1994). The construction of authorship: Textual appropriation in law and literature. Duke University Press.**

Jaszi and Woodmansee's edited volume brought together legal scholars, literary critics, and historians to interrogate the construction of the "author" as a legal and cultural figure. The book is foundational for the broader critical-IP movement and remains essential reading for understanding why the author-figure remains so contested. For IP candidates working on authorship as historically constructed, on the limits of the Romantic author for contemporary creative practice, and on the ways AI further destabilizes the author-figure, the volume is indispensable. Pair with Rose, Boyle, and Vats.
