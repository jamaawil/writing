*COMM pillar · PhD Year 1 · Fall 2028*

**Braided extracurricular reading**

#### Fiction
`COMM` **Oyler, L. (2021). Fake accounts. Catapult.**

Oyler's narrator discovers her boyfriend runs a conspiracy-theory Instagram account and flees to Berlin, where she creates fake dating profiles. The novel skewers internet sincerity, self-curation, and post-2016 political performance. Its method is acidic first-person essayistic fiction, drawing on the rhythms of online discourse. Fake Accounts dramatizes identity construction, conspiracy media, and the performative authenticity demands of platform life. For COMM/equity doctoral candidates, Oyler's novel pairs productively with disinformation scholarship (boyd, Phillips) and offers a granular account of how platform affordances reshape subjectivity, intimacy, and political belief.
`COMM` **Burgess, A. (1962). A clockwork orange. William Heinemann.**

Burgess's novel deploys an invented argot, Nadsat, to render his protagonist Alex's first-person narration of ultraviolence and state-imposed behavioral conditioning. The novel makes language itself the site of struggle between individual interiority and behavioral engineering. Its method is linguistic invention as ethical estrangement. A Clockwork Orange is essential for COMM/equity doctoral work on language, conditioning, and the rhetoric of rehabilitation. Burgess's Ludovico Technique prefigures contemporary debates about behavior modification through algorithmic feedback, with implications for AI policy and the ethics of recommender systems that nudge users toward platform-determined outcomes.
`COMM` **Ngugi wa Thiong'o. (1977). Petals of blood. Heinemann.**

Ngugi's novel of post-independence Kenya features a journalist, lawyer, teacher, and barmaid suspected of murder in a transforming town. The novel weaves news reportage, oral storytelling, and political tract into its texture. Its method is polyphonic Marxist-realist novel. Petals of Blood is essential for COMM/equity doctoral work on postcolonial communication, the persistence of oral traditions, and the role of journalism in political struggle. Ngugi models communication as embedded in material and political economy, anticipating Couldry and Mejias's analysis of data colonialism in contemporary platform environments.
`COMM` **Roy, A. (1997). The god of small things. Random House.**

Roy's novel of the Ipe family in Kerala braids personal and political tragedy through highly mediated forms of communication: love letters, court proceedings, news reports, and the spectral genre of "love laws" governing whom Indians may speak with intimately. Its method is non-linear, lyrical, deeply intertextual. The God of Small Things is essential for COMM/equity doctoral work on caste, gendered speech regulation, and the silences inscribed in family communication. Roy models how literary form (recursive narration, fragmentation) can render trauma that resists straightforward communication, an insight relevant to media studies of representation.
`COMM` **Morrison, T. (1977). Song of Solomon. Alfred A. Knopf.**

Morrison's novel of Milkman Dead's search for his ancestral story features oral tradition, family secrets, and a chase across the American South. Its method is mythic realism deeply infused with African American storytelling traditions. Song of Solomon is essential for COMM/equity doctoral work on oral tradition as historical archive, naming as communicative practice, and the politics of inherited story. Morrison models how communicative tradition encodes survival knowledge across generations, relevant to contemporary debates about indigenous communication theory and the limits of computational archives.
#### Nonfiction
`COMM` **Castells, M. (1996). The rise of the network society. Blackwell.**

Castells's foundational trilogy opens with this analysis of how networked information capitalism reorganizes economy, work, and identity. His method is comparative-empirical political sociology of networks. The Rise of the Network Society remains essential reading for COMM doctoral candidates, providing the macro framework for understanding networked communication's transformation of social structure. For COMM/AI/equity candidates, Castells's network analysis offers a methodological resource for thinking about platform power that complements more critical political-economic accounts (Zuboff, Srnicek).
`COMM` **Brock, A. (2020). Distributed Blackness: African American cybercultures. NYU Press.**

Brock's foundational study develops "Critical Technocultural Discourse Analysis" (CTDA) to analyze Black Twitter, Afrofuturism, and Black gaming cultures. His method is critical race media studies combining technology studies with linguistic and cultural analysis. Distributed Blackness is essential reading for COMM/equity/AI doctoral work, providing the most rigorous account of Black digital practice as world-making rather than mere participation. For doctoral candidates working at the platform/equity intersection, Brock offers methodological resources for analyzing minoritized digital communities on their own epistemological terms.
`COMM` **Nissenbaum, H. (2010). Privacy in context: Technology, policy, and the integrity of social life. Stanford Law Books.**

Nissenbaum's foundational study develops "contextual integrity" as the theoretical framework for privacy in networked environments. Her method is philosophical-legal theory of privacy. Privacy in Context is essential reading for COMM/IP/AI doctoral work. For doctoral candidates, Nissenbaum's contextual-integrity framework offers the most rigorous philosophical analytic for analyzing privacy harms in AI training, platform data extraction, and biometric surveillance.
`COMM` **Jenkins, H. (2006). Convergence culture: Where old and new media collide. NYU Press.**

Jenkins's foundational study develops "convergence culture" as the analytic for how participatory audiences interact across media platforms. His method is participatory cultural studies. Convergence Culture is essential reading for COMM/IP doctoral work on audience studies. For doctoral candidates, Jenkins's analytic of participatory culture remains generative even as later scholars (Gillespie, Couldry) critique its optimism, providing essential context for current platform debates about creator labor and copyright.
`COMM` **Bratton, B. H. (2015). The stack: On software and sovereignty. MIT Press.**

Bratton's foundational theoretical study analyzes contemporary planetary computation as "the Stack," composed of layers from Earth to Cloud to User. His method is speculative geopolitical theory of computation. The Stack is essential reading for COMM/AI doctoral work on the geopolitics of platforms. For doctoral candidates, Bratton provides the most ambitious theoretical framework for thinking about platforms as planetary infrastructure with implications for sovereignty.

#### Fiction
`EQ` **Orwell, G. (1949). Nineteen eighty-four. Secker & Warburg.**

Orwell's canonical surveillance dystopia gives critical race-and-tech scholarship a vocabulary—Big Brother, doublethink, Newspeak—still central to discussions of algorithmic governance and information warfare. Winston Smith's negotiation of pervasive observation, falsified records, and language manipulation anticipates concerns about deepfakes, content moderation, and the politics of memory in platform societies. While Orwell focuses on state power, contemporary scholars extend his framework to corporate platforms whose telemetric reach exceeds the Party's. Doctoral readers should treat 1984 as a foundational reference text against which to read Zuboff's Surveillance Capitalism and Browne's Dark Matters.
`EQ` **Butler, O. E. (1993). Parable of the sower. Four Walls Eight Windows.**

Butler's prophetic novel follows Lauren Olamina, a young Black woman with hyperempathy syndrome, as she leads a community north through climate collapse, privatized policing, and predatory labor regimes in 2020s California. Butler anticipates the gig economy, militarized borders, biometric surveillance, and the rise of charismatic authoritarianism. Olamina's emergent religion, Earthseed, theorizes change as the only constant, a counter-doctrine to optimization fantasies. For AI-equity scholars, Butler is indispensable: she centers Black women's leadership, builds collective survival epistemologies, and refuses techno-determinist despair. The novel pairs powerfully with Ruha Benjamin's work on abolitionist imagination.
`EQ` **Butler, O. E. (1988). Adulthood rites. Warner Books.**

The second Xenogenesis novel follows Akin, a hybrid child, navigating tensions between Oankali and resister humans. Butler explores what it means to belong to multiple lineages and to advocate for those refused full personhood. The novel speaks directly to contemporary debates about racial categorization in AI systems, mixed-race subjectivity, and the politics of refusal. Akin's advocacy for resister humans models a politics of solidarity across difference that AI-ethics frameworks routinely struggle to articulate.
`EQ` **Okorafor, N. (2020). Remote control. Tordotcom.**

In near-future Ghana, Sankofa, "the adopted daughter of death," wanders dispensing lethal touch after a meteor shard infuses her with strange power. Okorafor weaves global tech infrastructures—LifeGen Technologies, communication networks—through Ghanaian rural life, asking how multinational extraction and folkloric resonance coexist. For doctoral readers, the novel is a compact meditation on data colonialism, biotech extraction, and the embodied costs of becoming legible to global systems.
`EQ` **Jemisin, N. K. (2018). How long 'til Black future month? Orbit.**

Jemisin's story collection gathers speculative pieces ranging from post-Katrina New Orleans to a pre-emancipation parallel world. Several stories engage directly with computing, surveillance, and embodied algorithmic harm. The titular essay theorizes why Black futures have been so systematically erased from speculative imagination, anticipating Ruha Benjamin's call to dream beyond the New Jim Code. The collection is ideal for seminar reading because of its tonal range and accessible entry points.
#### Nonfiction
`EQ` **Costanza-Chock, S. (2020). Design justice: Community-led practices to build the worlds we need. MIT Press.**

Costanza-Chock develops design justice as a framework rooted in the Design Justice Network's principles, demanding that technology be built with and by marginalized communities. Drawing on years of practice with movement organizations, the book theorizes participatory methods that center disabled, queer, trans, Black, Indigenous, and feminist-of-color knowledges. For AI-equity readers, Costanza-Chock offers both critique and constructive methodology—essential for doctoral students aiming to translate scholarship into practice.
`EQ` **Birhane, A. (2021). Algorithmic injustice: A relational ethics approach. Patterns, 2(2), 100205.**

Birhane develops relational ethics, drawing on African philosophy and feminist STS, to argue that algorithmic harms cannot be fixed by individual-level fairness adjustments. Her work, alongside coauthored papers on data audits and Big Tech capture, has reshaped the AI ethics conversation. For doctoral readers, Birhane's body of work models rigorous methodological intervention from a Global South perspective.
`EQ` **Crawford, K. (2021). Atlas of AI: Power, politics, and the planetary costs of artificial intelligence. Yale University Press.**

Crawford reframes AI as a planetary extractive industry, tracing lithium mining, data labor, classification systems, and military applications. The book insists AI is "neither artificial nor intelligent" but rather material, embodied, and political. Essential reading for any AI-equity course.
`EQ` **Davis, A. Y. (2003). Are prisons obsolete? Seven Stories Press.**

Davis develops abolitionist argument in clear, accessible prose, drawing on her own activist and scholarly experience. For AI-equity readers, Are Prisons Obsolete? is essential context for any work on carceral technology, predictive policing, and the prison-industrial-AI complex.
`EQ` **Said, E. W. (1978). Orientalism. Pantheon Books.**

Said's foundational text theorizes how Western academic and popular discourse produced "the Orient" as object of knowledge and domination. For AI-equity scholars, Said is essential for understanding how representational regimes—including those embedded in training data—encode geopolitical power.

**Papers (50)**

**1. James W. Carey, 1989 (essay orig. 1975).** *A Cultural Approach to Communication* canonical pre-1990
Communication as Culture: Essays on Media and Society (Routledge), Ch. 1, pp. 13–36

> Distinguishes transmission and ritual models of communication, grounding media studies in cultural meaning-making rather than information transfer.

**2. Stuart Hall, 1980.** *Encoding/Decoding* canonical pre-1990
Culture, Media, Language, ed. Hall et al. (Hutchinson), pp. 128–138

> Proposes that media messages are encoded with dominant meanings but audiences decode them through preferred, negotiated, or oppositional positions.

**3. Jürgen Habermas, 1964 (trans. 1974).** *The Public Sphere: An Encyclopedia Article* canonical pre-1990
New German Critique, No. 3 (Autumn 1974), pp. 49–55

> Defines the bourgeois public sphere as a discursive realm mediating state and civil society, establishing the normative benchmark for democratic communication.

**4. Nancy Fraser, 1990.** *Rethinking the Public Sphere: A Contribution to the Critique of Actually Existing Democracy* canonical pre-1990
Social Text, No. 25/26, pp. 56–80

> Critiques Habermas's single-sphere model and advances the concept of subaltern counterpublics as sites of contestation by excluded groups.

**5. Marshall McLuhan, 1964.** *The Medium is the Message* canonical pre-1990
Understanding Media: The Extensions of Man (McGraw-Hill), Ch. 1, pp. 7–21

> Argues that the formal properties of any communication medium — not its content — are the primary determinant of social and perceptual change.

**6. Manuel Castells, 1996.** *The Rise of the Network Society (Introduction and Chapter 1)* established 1990-2010
The Rise of the Network Society (Blackwell), Vol. I, pp. 1–70

> Theorizes informationalism and the space of flows as the structural logic of late-twentieth-century capitalism, reshaping communication, labor, and power.

**7. Nick Couldry and Andreas Hepp, 2017.** *The Mediated Construction of Reality (Introduction)* contemporary 2011-2025
The Mediated Construction of Reality (Polity Press), pp. 1–28

> Synthesizes mediatization theory with social constructivism to argue that media infrastructures now co-produce social reality, not merely represent it.

**8. James Boyle, 2003.** *The Second Enclosure Movement and the Construction of the Public Domain* established 1990-2010
Law and Contemporary Problems, Vol. 66, No. 1/2, pp. 33–74

> Applies the enclosure metaphor to intellectual property expansion, arguing that over-propertization threatens the information commons analogous to the original enclosure of land.

Science, Vol. 280, No. 5364, pp. 698–701

**11. Madhavi Sunder, 2006.** *IP3* established 1990-2010

> Extends IP theory beyond economic efficiency to encompass human development, participation, and cultural recognition as distributional goals of intellectual property law.

Configuring the Networked Self (Yale University Press), pp. 1–28

**Braided extracurricular reading**

#### Fiction
`XD` **Beukes, L. (2010). Zoo City. Angry Robot.**

[Equity + Communication + AI] Zinzi December, a guilty Johannesburg woman magically bound to a sloth, hustles email scams and missing-person work in a city where the ethically compromised manifest animal familiars. Beukes layers Nigerian email fraud, music-industry IP rip-offs, refugee economies, and algorithmic detective work. The "animal" externalizes the social tagging that already exists in surveillance systems. A noir that synthesizes communication scams, post-apartheid inequity, and the soft AI of credit scores and platform reputation.
`XD` **Butler, O. E. (1993). Parable of the sower. Four Walls Eight Windows.**

[Equity + AI + Communication] In a 2024 of corporate enclaves, climate collapse, and pyromaniac drug users, Lauren Olamina founds Earthseed. Butler binds religious authorship (a new scripture written by a Black teenage girl), surveillance and gated inequity, and proto-platform networks of message-bearing. The pillars meet in Earthseed: The Books of the Living as both communication act and proprietary canon-in-formation. A foundational cross-pillar dystopia.
`XD` **Smith, Z. (2000). White teeth. Hamish Hamilton.**

[Equity + AI + Communication] Smith's debut tracks three multiracial London families across decades, climaxing in a genetics conference and an animal-rights ambush. The pillars synthesize in the FutureMouse, a genetically engineered animal-as-IP, the family-as-communication-system across colonial diaspora, and the inequity politics of post-empire Britain. The pillars meet in the cross-cutting climax. Foundational early-twenty-first-century cross-pillar novel.
`XD` **Kushner, R. (2018). The Mars Room. Scribner.**

[Equity + Communication] A woman serving two life sentences in a California women's prison narrates the conditions of carceral life. Kushner binds the communication infrastructure of prison letters and bureaucracy to the equity politics of class, sex work, and incarceration. The pillars meet in the protagonist's letters and refused letters.
`XP` **Coetzee, J. M. (1980). Waiting for the barbarians. Secker & Warburg.**

Illuminates Equity/Communication: Coetzee's novel of an aging magistrate in a colonial outpost interrogating an Indigenous girl is a study in the epistemology of empire. Placed beside critical scholarship on biometrics and border surveillance, Waiting for the Barbarians illuminates how the colonial state produces its own enemies through information practices.
#### Nonfiction
`XD` **Zuboff, S. (2019). The age of surveillance capitalism: The fight for a human future at the new frontier of power. PublicAffairs.**

[Communication + Equity + AI] Zuboff theorizes the new economic logic of behavioral surplus: platforms extract data from human behavior to predict and modify it. The pillars synthesize through the platform's communication architecture as a site of inequity-producing AI training. A definitive cross-pillar text of the decade.
`XD` **Roberts, S. T. (2019). Behind the screen: Content moderation in the shadows of social media. Yale University Press.**

[Equity + Communication + AI] Roberts documents the invisible labor of commercial content moderators in the Philippines, India, and the U.S. The pillars synthesize in the moderation queue: a communication infrastructure built on global labor inequity that trains tomorrow's classifier AI.
`XD` **Halpern, O. (2014). Beautiful data: A history of vision and reason since 1945. Duke University Press.**

[AI + Communication + Equity] Halpern traces the history of cybernetic visualization and the design of "smart" environments. The pillars synthesize in the cybernetic eye.
`XD` **Mignolo, W. D. (2011). The darker side of Western modernity: Global futures, decolonial options. Duke University Press.**

[Equity + Communication] Mignolo binds the colonial-modern world system to communication and epistemology. The pillars synthesize in the call to "decolonial" thought.
`XP` **Chalmers, D. J. (1996). The conscious mind: In search of a fundamental theory. Oxford University Press.**

Illuminates AI: Chalmers's account of the "hard problem" is a foundational cross-pollination text for AI-consciousness scholarship.
