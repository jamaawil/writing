### COMM-510 — COMM 510 · Rhetoric, Argument & Persuasion

*COMM pillar · MA Year 1 · Spring 2027*

A graduate-level rhetoric course covering classical foundations (Aristotle, Cicero), the New Rhetoric (Perelman, Burke), contemporary feminist and decolonial rhetorical theory, and rhetoric of science and technology.

**Braided extracurricular reading**

#### Fiction
`COMM` **Pynchon, T. (1973). Gravity's rainbow. Viking Press.**

Pynchon's encyclopedic novel of WWII rocketry doubles as a treatise on communication: paranoid signals, encrypted messages, conditioning experiments, and the V-2 as both weapon and meaning-making apparatus. The novel renders Pavlovian behaviorism, ballistic mathematics, and corporate cartels as overlapping communication systems. Its method is hallucinatory polyphony. For COMM scholars, Gravity's Rainbow models how warfare, science, and media converge in what Friedrich Kittler would later theorize as discourse networks. Its relevance to contemporary AI debates lies in its early dramatization of how technical systems shape subjectivity, anticipating Zuboff's instrumentarian power and the algorithmic governance critiqued by Eubanks and Noble.
`COMM` **Le Guin, U. K. (1971). The lathe of heaven. Charles Scribner's Sons.**

Le Guin's novel imagines George Orr, whose dreams alter reality, and a psychologist who weaponizes Orr's gift to remake society. The novel raises questions about authorial intention, communication of subjective worlds, and the ethics of reality-construction. Its method is philosophical SF infused with Taoist sensibility. The Lathe of Heaven speaks to contemporary COMM/AI debates about generative model authorship, simulated worlds, and who gets to author shared realities. For doctoral candidates working at the equity intersection, Le Guin's careful attention to how Orr's modest, often racialized utopian visions get colonized by a powerful interpreter remains a foundational parable about epistemic injustice in communication.
`COMM` **boyd, d. (2014). It's complicated: The social lives of networked teens. Yale University Press.**

boyd's ethnography of teenage networked life provides a foundational corrective to moral panics about social media use. Drawing on years of fieldwork, the book argues that platforms are not corrupting youth but providing new architectures for ancient developmental tasks: identity formation, peer connection, navigating public/private boundaries. Methodologically, the work models how to study mediated practices ethnographically rather than through aggregate platform metrics. For COMM scholars, It's Complicated is essential reading on networked publics, drama dynamics, and the labor of identity performance online, with direct relevance to algorithmic content moderation and the contemporary discourse on youth and AI.
`COMM` **García Márquez, G. (1967). One hundred years of solitude (G. Rabassa, Trans.). Harper & Row. (Original work published 1967)**

García Márquez's foundational magical realist novel of the Buendía family in Macondo features Melquíades, whose parchments contain the family's entire prophesied history. Communication infrastructure (the railroad, the banana company's broadcasts, government repression of the massacre's record) shape Macondo's history. Its method is panoramic magical realism. One Hundred Years of Solitude is essential for COMM/equity doctoral work on collective memory, the erasure of inconvenient histories (the banana massacre), and the genres of historical communication. García Márquez's depiction of how state and corporate power rewrite catastrophe prefigures platform-era debates about archive, denial, and historical revisionism.
`COMM` **Yanagihara, H. (2015). A little life. Doubleday.**

Yanagihara's epic novel of four friends in New York centers on Jude, whose traumatic past resists full communication even to his closest intimates. Therapy, friendship correspondence, and selective revelation structure the narrative. Its method is intimate close third-person across decades. A Little Life is essential for COMM/equity doctoral work on trauma's communicative impossibility, the genre of confession, and the limits of testimonial recovery. Yanagihara models communication as ethical relation rather than information transfer, a theme with implications for AI-mediated mental health interventions and the limits of computational empathy.
#### Nonfiction
`COMM` **Anderson, B. (1983). Imagined communities: Reflections on the origin and spread of nationalism. Verso.**

Anderson's foundational claim that nations are imagined communities sustained by print capitalism (the novel, the newspaper) inaugurates a generation of scholarship on the relation between media and political belonging. His method is comparative historical political theory. Imagined Communities is essential reading for COMM doctoral work on media and politics. For COMM/AI/equity candidates, Anderson's print-capitalism framework offers a generative analogue for analyzing how platform capitalism constitutes new (and disrupts old) imagined communities, themes Castells, Couldry, and Bratton extend.

Suzor's foundational legal study analyzes how platform terms of service function as a private law regime governing billions of users without democratic accountability. Crawford's foundational study analyzes AI's material and political economies, from lithium mining to data labor to electrical infrastructure. Atlas of AI is essential reading for COMM/AI/equity doctoral work, providing the foundational materialist analytic for AI critique. **Braided extracurricular reading**

#### Fiction
`EQ` **Atwood, M. (2003). Oryx and Crake. McClelland and Stewart.**

The first novel in Atwood's MaddAddam trilogy follows Snowman/Jimmy in a post-pandemic world engineered by corporate biotech. Atwood scrutinizes how unregulated genetic and algorithmic optimization, driven by private capital, produces both new species and new precarities. The compound/pleeblands divide dramatizes the spatial logic of surveillance capitalism, where elite enclaves benefit from data extraction performed on marginalized populations outside the walls. The novel is indispensable for AI-equity scholarship because it links biotech, platform power, and ecological collapse to questions of who counts as human. Atwood's satire of techno-utopianism prefigures critiques by Zuboff, Benjamin, and Couldry.
`EQ` **Huxley, A. (1932). Brave new world. Chatto & Windus.**

Huxley imagines a society stratified by biological caste—Alphas to Epsilons—maintained by chemical pacification, behavioral conditioning, and consumer pleasure. Unlike Orwell's repressive state, Huxley's dystopia rules through engineered desire, anticipating the soft coercions of recommendation algorithms and platform-mediated affect. The novel offers AI-equity scholars a framework for thinking about consent under manufactured preference, the racialization of labor castes, and the ideology of optimization. Read alongside Eubanks and Noble, Brave New World exposes how technological "improvement" can naturalize hierarchy and depoliticize inequality.
`EQ` **Butler, O. E. (1979). Kindred. Doubleday.**

Dana, a Black woman in 1976 Los Angeles, is repeatedly pulled back to a Maryland plantation in the early nineteenth century to save her white ancestor. Butler uses time travel to insist on the material continuities of racial violence and to render embodied the historical conditions structuring contemporary technological systems. For equity scholars, Kindred operates as theory: it shows how present infrastructures—legal, economic, technological—are sedimented from past extraction. The novel anchors any reading list bridging Black studies, surveillance studies, and AI critique.
`EQ` **Okorafor, N. (2010). Who fears death. DAW Books.**

Set in a post-apocalyptic Saharan Africa, Okorafor's novel follows Onyesonwu, an Ewu (mixed-race) sorceress confronting genocidal violence and patriarchal control. The novel critiques how technologies of war—including weaponized rape—are deployed against marked bodies. Okorafor draws on Sudanese cosmology, refusing Western generic conventions and centering African epistemologies. For scholars working on decolonial AI and Black feminist thought, Who Fears Death models how speculative fiction can hold trauma, magic, and political analysis simultaneously.
`EQ` **Jemisin, N. K. (2016). The obelisk gate. Orbit.**

The second Broken Earth volume deepens questions of inheritance, resistance, and the ethics of using inherited tools of oppression. Essun's reunion with her daughter Nassun stages two competing theories of survival: refusal versus mastery. Jemisin's narration—largely in second person—implicates the reader in the violence she narrates, modeling the embodied stakes of refusing complicity. The novel is a powerful resource for thinking through critical-race-AI questions of repair, abolition, and complicity.
#### Nonfiction
`EQ` **Benjamin, R. (2019). Race after technology: Abolitionist tools for the New Jim Code. Polity Press.**

Benjamin argues that contemporary technologies often reinforce racial hierarchies under the guise of objectivity—what she calls the "New Jim Code." Drawing on Michelle Alexander's framing and Black feminist thought, Benjamin develops a toolkit for analyzing how design choices, training data, and infrastructure encode racial logics. Chapters interrogate predictive policing, automated risk assessment, and seemingly innocuous platform design. The book closes with abolitionist imagination as methodological commitment. For doctoral readers, Benjamin's text is essential—theoretically generative, empirically grounded, and pedagogically clear.
`EQ` **Zuboff, S. (2019). The age of surveillance capitalism. PublicAffairs.**

Zuboff names surveillance capitalism as the dominant economic logic of contemporary platforms, defining it as the unilateral claiming of human experience as raw material for behavioral prediction products. The book traces Google's invention of this logic and its diffusion across industries. While critiqued for under-theorizing race and the Global South, Surveillance Capitalism remains foundational vocabulary for AI-equity scholars.
`EQ` **Wajcman, J. (2004). TechnoFeminism. Polity Press.**

Wajcman synthesizes feminist STS to theorize how technology and gender mutually constitute one another. The book traces shifts from liberal feminist arguments about access, through cyberfeminist celebrations of cyborg politics, to material feminist engagements with infrastructure. For AI-equity readers, Wajcman is essential historical grounding for contemporary feminist tech scholarship.
`EQ` **Crenshaw, K. W. (1991). Mapping the margins: Intersectionality, identity politics, and violence against women of color. Stanford Law Review, 43(6), 1241–1299.**

Crenshaw's foundational essay introduces intersectionality as a framework for understanding how legal and political categories fail women of color. For AI-equity scholars, intersectionality is indispensable for thinking about classification harms in automated systems.
`EQ` **Foucault, M. (1977). Discipline and punish: The birth of the prison (A. Sheridan, Trans.). Pantheon Books.**

Foucault's classic analyzes how modern power produces docile bodies through surveillance, classification, and discipline. While Foucault's framework requires supplementation from Black studies and decolonial theory, Discipline and Punish remains foundational vocabulary—panopticon, biopower, governmentality—for surveillance studies.

`IP` **Rothschild, H. (2015). The improbability of love. Bloomsbury.**

Rothschild's satire moves a small Watteau painting through London's art world, attaching its narrative to dealers, restorers, oligarchs, and Holocaust restitution claimants. Kuang dissects every mechanism by which Hayward launders the appropriation: copyediting, marketing, identity-claiming, social-media performance. The novel anticipates almost every conceptual problem raised by generative training corpora: who owns voice, what proof of origination looks like, and how a publishing industry profits from cultural mimicry without accountability. For research on equity in IP law and the racial economy of authorship, Yellowface is a primary text, not a peripheral one.

For IP researchers working on archives, copyright in unpublished works, and the duration debate that culminates in Eldred v. Ashcroft, Byatt offers a generative literary frame. **Braided extracurricular reading**

#### Fiction
`COMM` **Pynchon, T. (1990). Vineland. Little, Brown.**

Pynchon's California novel braids 1960s counterculture with 1980s Reagan-era surveillance. Television (the "Tube") functions as the novel's omnipresent communications environment, structuring memory, intimacy, and politics. The Thanatoids, ghosts of the war on drugs, watch endless TV in spectral suspension. Its method is comic-political satire layered with melodrama. Vineland is essential reading for COMM scholars interested in how broadcast television organized late-twentieth-century American consciousness, and how that organization presaged platform-era attention capture. The novel's treatment of FBI infiltration, archives, and informants resonates with contemporary surveillance studies (Browne, Zuboff) for doctoral work at the COMM/equity intersection.
`COMM` **Le Guin, U. K. (1974). The dispossessed. Harper & Row.**

Le Guin's anarchist Annarres and capitalist Urras present rival communication regimes: Annaresti language structurally inhibits possessive thought; Urrasti media saturate consumer subjectivity. Physicist Shevek's transit between worlds dramatizes language ideologies in action. Its method is comparative ethnographic SF. The Dispossessed is essential for COMM doctoral work on language, ideology, and political economy. Le Guin's invented Pravic language offers a thought-experiment in linguistic relativity directly relevant to current debates about LLM training, language equity, and how AI systems encode dominant linguistic norms. Pairs powerfully with Bakhtinian dialogism and decolonial communication theory.
`COMM` **Hamid, M. (2007). The reluctant fundamentalist. Hamish Hamilton.**

Hamid's dramatic monologue features Changez, a Pakistani former Princeton graduate, addressing an unnamed American interlocutor in a Lahore café. The novel weaponizes second-person address to interrogate post-9/11 communicative ethics. Its method is hostile dramatic monologue. The Reluctant Fundamentalist is essential for COMM/equity doctoral work on cross-cultural rhetoric, racialized listening, and the politics of audience. Hamid models how form (apostrophe, ambiguity) becomes argument. For COMM/AI candidates, the novel raises questions about who gets read as suspicious, prefiguring debates about predictive policing and algorithmic risk-assessment of racialized speech.

`COMM` **Tan, A. (1989). The joy luck club. G. P. Putnam's Sons.**

Tan's novel features four Chinese American mother-daughter pairs whose stories interweave around a mahjong club. Cross-generational and cross-cultural communicative gaps structure the narrative. Its method is interlinked first-person narration across multiple perspectives. The Joy Luck Club is essential for COMM/equity doctoral work on gendered, generational, and cross-cultural communication, the politics of translation, and the work of literature in transmitting subaltern histories. Tan models how immigrant maternal storytelling functions as alternative archive, relevant to studies of oral history and the politics of what AI corpora preserve.
#### Nonfiction
`COMM` **Schudson, M. (1978). Discovering the news: A social history of American newspapers. Basic Books.**

Schudson's foundational sociology of American journalism traces the emergence of objectivity as a professional ideology in the late 19th-century press, against the older partisan model. His method is comparative historical sociology of journalism. Discovering the News remains essential reading for journalism studies and COMM doctoral work. For COMM/equity candidates, Schudson's analysis of objectivity as ideological achievement opens onto contemporary debates about journalistic neutrality, the racialized politics of "view from nowhere" reporting, and the platform-era crisis of journalism's authority.
`COMM` **Fraser, N. (1997). Justice interruptus: Critical reflections on the "postsocialist" condition. Routledge.**

Fraser's collected essays extend her foundational critique of Habermas's public sphere into a theory of "subaltern counter-publics" that contest dominant publics' exclusions. Her method is critical-theoretical political philosophy. Justice Interruptus is essential reading for COMM/equity doctoral work, providing the analytic of counter-publics that scholars from Squires (Black publics) to Warner (queer publics) extend. For platform-era candidates, Fraser's framework illuminates how platform-mediated counter-publics (Black Twitter, BookTok) operate, with implications for content moderation policy.
`COMM` **Vaidhyanathan, S. (2018). Antisocial media: How Facebook disconnects us and undermines democracy. Oxford University Press.**

Vaidhyanathan's foundational study analyzes Facebook's role in democratic erosion across the U.S., India, Myanmar, and the Philippines. His method is comparative platform critique drawing on political economy and media studies. Antisocial Media is essential reading for COMM/AI doctoral work on platform politics. For doctoral candidates, Vaidhyanathan pairs with Tufekci and Zuboff to constitute the foundational platform-critical literature for democratic theory.
`COMM` **Nakamura, L. (2002). Cybertypes: Race, ethnicity, and identity on the Internet. Routledge.**

Nakamura's foundational study analyzes how racial stereotypes ("cybertypes") proliferated on early text and graphic web. Her method is critical race media studies focused on visual and verbal stereotypes. Cybertypes is essential reading for COMM/equity doctoral work on race and digital media. For doctoral candidates, Nakamura's foundational work prefigures contemporary scholarship on algorithmic racism (Noble, Benjamin) by demonstrating how racialized representation has structured the internet from its earliest moments.
