**Papers (50)**

**1. Mark A. Lemley, 2008.** *The Surprising Virtues of Treating Trade Secrets as IP Rights* established 1990-2010
Stanford Law Review, vol. 61, pp. 311–353

> Argues that trade secret law functions as a coherent IP regime—not merely as tort or contract—by providing incentives to invest in secrecy that are distinct from patent incentives.

**2. Camilla A. Hrdy & Mark D. Seaman, 2022.** *Beyond the Trade Secret/Patent Dichotomy (verify)* contemporary 2011-2025
William & Mary Law Review, vol. 63, pp. 1 ff. (verify)

> Challenges the binary framing of trade secret vs. patent protection by examining hybrid strategies and the doctrinal blurring between the two regimes.

**3. Robert G. Bone, 1998.** *A New Look at Trade Secret Law: Doctrine in Search of Justification* established 1990-2010
California Law Review, vol. 86, pp. 241–313

> Provides a comprehensive doctrinal critique of trade secret law, questioning whether existing justifications—property, tort, and contract—are internally coherent.

**4. Michael Risch, 2007.** *Why Do We Have Trade Secrets?* established 1990-2010
Marquette Intellectual Property Law Review, vol. 11, pp. 1–76

> Surveys the normative justifications for trade secret protection—labor, property, efficiency, and fairness—and assesses which best explains current doctrine.

**5. David S. Levine, 2013.** *Secrecy and Unaccountability: Trade Secrets in Our Public Infrastructure* contemporary 2011-2025
Florida Law Review, vol. 59, pp. 135–194 (verify)

> Examines how trade secret claims shielding public-infrastructure software undermine democratic accountability and due process.

**6. James Pooley, 2013.** *The Myth of the Trade Secret Troll: Why the DTSA Threatens to Destabilize the Balance of Power Between Employers and Employees* contemporary 2011-2025
George Mason Law Review, vol. 23, pp. 1175–1213 (verify)

> Critically evaluates employee mobility and the expanding scope of trade secret law prior to the DTSA, identifying structural power imbalances between employers and workers.

**7. Sharon K. Sandeen & Christopher B. Seaman, 2017.** *Toward a Federal Jurisprudence of Trade Secret Law* contemporary 2011-2025
Berkeley Technology Law Journal, vol. 32, pp. 829–908

> Analyzes the first year of DTSA litigation to map emerging federal common law of trade secrets and divergences from state law baselines.

**8. Elizabeth A. Rowe, 2017.** *Saving Trade Secret Disclosures on the Internet Through Sequential Preservation* contemporary 2011-2025
Ohio State Law Journal, vol. 78, pp. 671–726 (verify)

> Proposes a procedural framework for preserving trade secret status when disclosures occur online, addressing gaps the DTSA did not resolve.

**9. Eric Goldman, 2016 (verify).** *Trade Secrets and Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act (verify)* contemporary 2011-2025
George Mason Law Review (verify)

> Explores the interaction between § 230 immunity and trade secret claims against online platforms, arguing that § 230 limits civil liability in ways trade secret doctrine has not fully absorbed.

**10. David S. Levine, 2018.** *Secrecy and the Unaccountable Algorithm (verify)* contemporary 2011-2025
Administrative Law Review (verify), vol. 70 (verify)

> Argues that trade secret shields on government-deployed algorithms obstruct administrative-law accountability mechanisms, requiring a public-interest exception.

**11. David S. Almeling, Darin W. Snyder, Michael Sapoznikow, Whitney E. McCollum & Ji.** *A Statistical Analysis of Trade Secret Litigation in Federal Courts* established 1990-2010
Gonzaga Law Review, vol. 45, pp. 291–334 (verify)

> Provides the first large-scale empirical analysis of federal trade secret litigation, revealing patterns in industry distribution, case outcomes, and damages.

**12. Christopher B. Seaman, 2015.** *The Case Against Federalizing Trade Secrecy* contemporary 2011-2025
Virginia Law Review, vol. 101, pp. 317–390

> Makes the pre-DTSA case that federal preemption of state trade secret law would distort the incremental innovation system and harm employee mobility.

**13. Sharon K. Sandeen, 2010.** *Kewanee Revisited: Returning to First Principles of Intellectual Property Law to Determine the Issue of Federal Preemption* established 1990-2010
Marquette Intellectual Property Law Review, vol. 14, pp. 1–68 (verify)

> Revisits the Supreme Court's Kewanee Oil framework to recalibrate how federal IP preemption should apply to state trade secret regimes.

**14. Rebecca Wexler, 2018.** *Life, Liberty, and Trade Secrets: Intellectual Property in the Criminal Justice System* contemporary 2011-2025
Stanford Law Review, vol. 70, pp. 1343–1429

> Demonstrates that trade secret claims over criminal-justice algorithms deprive defendants of the procedural rights necessary to challenge evidence, colliding with constitutional due process guarantees. [REQUIRED]

**15. Amanda Levendowski, 2018.** *How Copyright Law Can Fix Artificial Intelligence's Implicit Bias Problem* contemporary 2011-2025
Washington Law Review, vol. 93, pp. 579–630

> Proposes using copyright's fair-use doctrine to compel disclosure of biased training datasets as a mechanism for correcting AI discrimination.

**16. Frank Pasquale, 2010.** *Beyond Innovation and Competition: The Need for Qualified Transparency in Internet Intermediaries* established 1990-2010
Northwestern University Law Review, vol. 104, pp. 105–173

> Develops the concept of "qualified transparency" to navigate the tension between trade secret protection for platform algorithms and the public interest in algorithmic accountability.

**17. Danielle Keats Citron, 2008.** *Technological Due Process* established 1990-2010
Washington University Law Review, vol. 85, pp. 1249–1313

> Identifies how automated government decision-making systems deprive individuals of meaningful procedural protections and calls for due-process design requirements in public algorithms.

**18. Danielle Keats Citron & Frank Pasquale, 2014.** *The Scored Society: Due Process for Automated Predictions* contemporary 2011-2025
Washington Law Review, vol. 89, pp. 1–33

> Proposes a procedural-due-process framework for automated scoring systems used in consequential decisions such as employment and credit.

**19. Kiel Brennan-Marquez, 2017.** *Plausible Cause": Explanatory Standards in the Age of Powerful Machines* contemporary 2011-2025
Vanderbilt Law Review, vol. 70, pp. 1249–1302 (verify)

> Argues that evidentiary opacity in machine-learning decision systems undermines the "plausible cause" threshold in Fourth Amendment analysis, requiring new explanation standards.

**20. Rebecca Wexler, 2020.** *Privacy as Intellectual Property? (verify)* contemporary 2011-2025
Stanford Law Review, vol. 72, pp. 1(verify)

> Interrogates whether trade secret and privacy law are converging in their treatment of sensitive datasets, arguing that treating personal data as proprietary IP exacerbates inequality.

**21. Pamela Samuelson, 1999.** *Intellectual Property and the Digital Economy: Why the Anti-Circumvention Regulations Need to Be Revised* established 1990-2010
Berkeley Technology Law Journal, vol. 14, pp. 519–566

> Offers an early systematic critique of DMCA § 1201's anti-circumvention regime, warning it would impede security research, fair use, and interoperability.

**22. R. Anthony Reese, 2003.** *Will Merging Access Controls and Rights Controls Undermine the Structure of Anticircumvention Law?* established 1990-2010
Berkeley Technology Law Journal, vol. 18, pp. 619–666

> Identifies a structural ambiguity in § 1201 between access controls and rights controls, predicting doctrinal instability when the two categories merge in practice.

**23. Fred von Lohmann, 2002.** *Unintended Consequences: Twelve Years Under the DMCA* established 1990-2010
Electronic Frontier Foundation White Paper (updated 2010)

> Documents empirical harms from DMCA § 1201 enforcement—chilling effects on security research, competition, and consumer rights—over the first twelve years of the statute.

**24. Shyamkrishna Balganesh, 2009.** *Foreseeability and Copyright Incentives* established 1990-2010
Harvard Law Review, vol. 122, pp. 1569–1637

> Proposes a foreseeability doctrine to limit anti-circumvention's extension into markets copyright originally had no intention of reaching, connecting § 1201 to underlying IP incentives.

**25. Aaron Perzanowski & Jason Schultz, 2011.** *Digital Exhaustion* contemporary 2011-2025
UCLA Law Review, vol. 58, pp. 889–946

> Argues that the first-sale doctrine should apply to digital goods, directly challenging anti-circumvention enforcement that prevents resale and transfer of digital media.

**26. Séverine Dusollier, 2003.** *Exceptions and Technological Measures in the European Copyright Directive of 2001 — An Empty Promise* established 1990-2010
International Review of Intellectual Property and Competition Law, vol. 34, pp. 62–75 (verify)

> Evaluates the EU Information Society Directive's technological protection measure provisions and concludes that exceptions for users are rendered hollow by the architecture of enforcement.

**27. Molly Shaffer Van Houweling, 2005.** *Author Autonomy and Atomism in Copyright Law* established 1990-2010
Virginia Law Review, vol. 96, pp. 549–636 (verify)

> Links anti-circumvention doctrine to a broader argument that technological locks fragment the copyright system by enabling hyper-granular licensing that displaces statutory balances.

**28. Orin S. Kerr, 2003.** *Cybercrime's Scope: Interpreting "Access" and "Authorization" in Computer Misuse Statutes* established 1990-2010
New York University Law Review, vol. 78, pp. 1596–1668

> Provides the canonical analysis of "access" and "authorization" under the CFAA, identifying ambiguity between code-based and contract-based interpretations that shapes all subsequent litigation. [REQUIRED]

**29. Orin S. Kerr, 2010.** *Vagueness Challenges to the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act* established 1990-2010
Minnesota Law Review, vol. 94, pp. 1561–1595

> Applies void-for-vagueness doctrine to the CFAA, concluding that the statute's breadth creates constitutional problems that neither courts nor Congress have adequately addressed.

**30. Danielle Keats Citron & Woodrow Hartzog, 2015.** *The CFAA and What's Left of the Common Law (verify)* contemporary 2011-2025
Illinois Law Review (verify), vol. 2015 (verify)

> Examines whether CFAA preempts state tort claims for data breaches, arguing that common-law development has been stunted by federal statutory overreach.

**31. Eric Goldman, 2017 (verify).** *The Computer Fraud and Abuse Act: A Problematic Law That Gets Misapplied Even More Than It Should Be (verify)* contemporary 2011-2025
Marquette Intellectual Property Law Review (verify)

> Catalogues judicial misapplications of CFAA to ordinary web-scraping and platform policy violations, arguing for a legislative reset of the "exceeds authorized access" standard.

**32. Jonathan Mayer, 2017.** *Cybercrime Litigation* contemporary 2011-2025
University of Pennsylvania Law Review, vol. 164, pp. 1453–1536 (verify)

> Empirically maps CFAA civil litigation, revealing that most suits are filed by private parties against insiders or competitors, not by the government against external hackers.

**33. Orin S. Kerr, 2022.** *Van Buren and the Limits of the CFAA* contemporary 2011-2025
Journal of Criminal Law and Criminology, vol. 112 (verify), pp. 1 ff. (verify)

> Analyzes the Supreme Court's Van Buren v. United States decision, evaluating how the "gates-up-or-down" reading of authorization clarifies but does not fully resolve the CFAA's scope.

**34. Andrea M. Matwyshyn, 2010.** *Hacking Speech: Informational Speech and the First Amendment* established 1990-2010
Northwestern University Law Review, vol. 107, pp. 795–874 (verify)

**35. J.H. Reichman & Pamela Samuelson, 1997.** *Intellectual Property Rights in Data?* established 1990-2010
Vanderbilt Law Review, vol. 50, pp. 51–166

**Braided extracurricular reading**

#### Fiction
`XD` **Nayler, R. (2022). The mountain in the sea. MCD/Farrar, Straus and Giroux.**

[AI + Equity + Communication] A marine biologist studying a newly-discovered intelligent octopus species off Vietnam works for a corporation also operating an AI-piloted slaver fishing fleet manned by trafficked workers. Nayler interlaces three pillars: non-human cognition and machine intelligence, the labor inequity of automated extraction, and the semiotics of inter-species communication. The novel insists that AI ethics is unintelligible without confronting marine slavery and indigenous knowledge of nonhuman minds. A philosophical thriller that refuses to discuss minds in isolation from political economy.
`XD` **Butler, O. E. (1998). Parable of the talents. Seven Stories Press.**

[Equity + Communication + AI] Earthseed grows into a movement under a Christian-nationalist administration's "Crusaders" wearing slave collars on women and other dissidents. Butler binds the carceral collar (a near-AI implant) to political communication and to the inequity of evangelical resurgence. The pillars meet in the daughter's documentary frame: an archival recovery of her mother's testimony.
`XD` **Smith, Z. (2012). NW. Hamish Hamilton.**

[Communication + Equity] Four Londoners from one council estate diverge into different class trajectories. Smith binds the formal experiment (chat transcripts, walking directions, list chapters) to the inequity of neighborhood. The pillars meet in the formal architecture: communication design as political argument about urban race and class.
`XD` **Otsuka, J. (2011). The Buddha in the attic. Alfred A. Knopf.**

[Equity + Communication] Otsuka's first-person plural narration follows Japanese picture brides arriving in California in the early 20th century. The choral voice itself is a communication theory of community under inequity: how many women's voices does it take to make one history. The pillars meet in the formal experiment.
`XP` **Coetzee, J. M. (1999). Disgrace. Secker & Warburg.**

Illuminates Communication: Coetzee's post-apartheid novel of a disgraced professor in rural South Africa is a study in what happens to communication after the moral consensus collapses. Placed beside contemporary platform polarization, the book illuminates how speech acts misfire across a transformed audience. A cross-pollination for thinking about cancel-culture discourse in its political-historical longue durée.
#### Nonfiction
`XD` **Bridle, J. (2018). New dark age: Technology and the end of the future. Verso.**

[AI + Communication + Equity] Bridle reads computation as a system that produces opacity even as it claims transparency, with consequences for climate, politics, and content. The pillars meet in the "automation paradox" and the algorithmic farming of YouTube Kids. A major popular-scholarly cross-pillar text.
`XD` **Gray, M. L., & Suri, S. (2019). Ghost work: How to stop Silicon Valley from building a new global underclass. Houghton Mifflin Harcourt.**

[Equity + AI + Communication] Gray and Suri document the workers behind the AI curtain—the click-workers, the labelers, the "ghosts" who make AI seem autonomous. The pillars synthesize in the figure of the data-labeler as both communication worker and AI substrate. A canonical text on AI-equity-labor.
`XD` **Larkin, B. (2008). Signal and noise: Media, infrastructure, and urban culture in Nigeria. Duke University Press.**

[Communication + Equity + IP] Larkin's ethnography of Nigerian media infrastructures binds Nollywood's IP improvisations, urban inequity, and communication politics. The pillars synthesize in the cable TV box.
`XD` **Couldry, N. (2010). Why voice matters: Culture and politics after neoliberalism. Sage.**

[Communication + Equity] Couldry argues that "voice" is the political ground neoliberalism cannot recognize. The pillars synthesize in the framework of voice as both communication and equity claim.
`XP` **Dennett, D. C. (1991). Consciousness explained. Little, Brown.**

Illuminates AI: Dennett's functionalist account is the indispensable counterweight to Searle in any AI-consciousness syllabus.
