### COMM-501 — COMM 501 · Communication Theory & Media Studies

*COMM pillar · MA Year 1 · Fall 2026*

A graduate survey of foundational communication theory and contemporary media studies, anchored in Carey's ritual-vs-transmission distinction and extending through platform studies. Builds the critical apparatus through which the rest of the program reads its objects.

- ★ **John Durham Peters** — *Speaking into the Air*
- ★ **Craig & Muller** — *Theorizing Communication*

- ★ **Stuart Hall** — *Essential Essays Vol. 1*

**Braided extracurricular reading**

#### Fiction
`COMM` **Pynchon, T. (1966). The crying of lot 49. J. B. Lippincott.**

Oedipa Maas inherits the estate of a real estate magnate and stumbles into the Tristero, a possibly fictional underground postal system. Pynchon's compact novel is a primer in semiotics, paranoia, and the limits of interpretation. Every signifier branches into recursive ambiguity. Its method is paranoid hermeneutics played for comedy. Lot 49 anticipates the COMM concerns of platform-era information environments: conspiracy as alternative epistemology, communication networks as power, and the impossibility of decoding without ideological commitment. For COMM/AI scholars studying disinformation and recommender-system rabbit holes, Pynchon's Trystero remains the founding parable.
`COMM` **Atwood, M. (1985). The handmaid's tale. McClelland & Stewart.**

Atwood's dystopia of Gilead is also a treatise on linguistic control: the Aunts' indoctrination, the prohibition on women's reading, the chant-along ceremonies. Communication restriction is the regime's central mechanism. Its method is first-person fragmentary narration, framed by a future academic conference parsing Offred's recovered cassette tapes. The Handmaid's Tale is essential for COMM/equity doctoral work on language, gender, censorship, and the archival politics of survivor testimony. Atwood's metafictional frame raises questions about whose voices reach posterity, prefiguring contemporary debates about platform de-platforming, content moderation, and equity in AI training corpora.
`COMM` **Ishiguro, K. (2021). Klara and the sun. Faber and Faber.**

Ishiguro's novel narrates from the perspective of Klara, an Artificial Friend, observing Josie, a sick child, and a society stratified by genetic enhancement. The novel is fundamentally about how an AI tries to interpret human communication. Its method is restricted-knowledge first-person narration. Klara and the Sun is essential for COMM/AI doctoral work on machine interpretation, the affective dimensions of AI companionship, and what Couldry and Hepp call mediatization. Ishiguro models how an artificial mind learns by observation, raising questions directly relevant to large language model training, embodied AI, and the ethics of synthetic relationships.
`COMM` **Saramago, J. (2004). Seeing (M. J. Costa, Trans.). Harcourt. (Original work published 2004)**

Saramago's sequel imagines an election in which most voters cast blank ballots; the government interprets this as conspiracy and besieges the city. Newspapers, manifestos, surveillance, and rumor all figure. Its method is political allegory in Saramago's signature prose. Seeing is essential for COMM doctoral candidates on electoral communication, the manufacture of crisis, and the rhetoric of legitimacy. For COMM/equity/AI work, Saramago's depiction of how state communication apparatus constructs "the people" as enemy is directly relevant to platform-era studies of polarization, ethnonationalism, and the algorithmic amplification of in-group/out-group rhetoric.
`COMM` **Otsuka, J. (2002). When the emperor was divine. Alfred A. Knopf.**

Otsuka's novel of a Japanese American family interned during WWII unfolds in spare, restrained chapters. The first chapter shows the mother reading the evacuation order; subsequent chapters track displacement, internment, and uneasy return. Its method is minimalist multi-perspectival realism. When the Emperor Was Divine is essential for COMM/equity doctoral work on state communication of racialized policy, the rhetorical work of euphemism (relocation, internment), and the politics of post-event archival recovery. Otsuka's restrained voice models how literary form can do the work of testimony when survivors are reluctant to speak.
#### Nonfiction
`COMM` **Hall, S. (with Morley, D., & Chen, K.-H.). (1996). Stuart Hall: Critical dialogues in cultural studies. Routledge.**

This collection assembles Hall's foundational essays on encoding/decoding, ideology, articulation theory, and Black diaspora. His method is conjunctural cultural analysis. The volume is essential for COMM doctoral work, providing Hall's full theoretical apparatus. Hall's encoding/decoding model, distinguishing dominant, negotiated, and oppositional readings, remains foundational for audience studies in the platform era. For COMM/equity doctoral candidates, Hall's articulation theory and analysis of Thatcherism offer methodological resources for analyzing platform-era ideological formations (alt-right, techno-libertarianism).
`COMM` **Williams, R. (1974). Television: Technology and cultural form. Schocken Books.**

Williams's foundational study develops the concept of "flow" to capture how television's structural form (continuous interrupted programming, rather than discrete works) shapes its cultural effects. His method is cultural materialism in dialogue with technology studies. Television remains essential reading for COMM doctoral work. For platform-era candidates, Williams's analysis of flow anticipates contemporary scholarship on social media feeds (Bucher, van Dijck), suggesting continuity rather than rupture between broadcast and platform forms of attention organization.
`COMM` **Pasquale, F. (2015). The black box society: The secret algorithms that control money and information. Harvard University Press.**

Pasquale's foundational legal-theoretical study analyzes how proprietary algorithms in finance, search, and social media constitute "black boxes" that resist regulatory and democratic accountability. His method is legal theory in dialogue with political economy. The Black Box Society is essential reading for COMM/IP/AI doctoral work, providing the foundational legal analytic of algorithmic opacity. For candidates working at the IP intersection, Pasquale's analysis of trade secrecy as legal infrastructure of algorithmic opacity is foundational.
`COMM` **Phillips, W., & Milner, R. M. (2021). You are here: A field guide for navigating polarized speech, conspiracy theories, and our polluted media landscape. MIT Press.**

Phillips and Milner's recent study develops a "field guide" for understanding contemporary disinformation as ecological problem rather than mere content problem. Their method is ecological media studies in dialogue with environmental humanities. You Are Here is essential reading for COMM doctoral work on platform-era disinformation. For doctoral candidates, the book provides a sophisticated alternative to misinformation/disinformation framings that pathologize individual content, offering ecological framings that implicate platform infrastructure.
`COMM` **Madianou, M., & Miller, D. (2012). Migration and new media: Transnational families and polymedia. Routledge.**

Madianou and Miller's foundational study draws on multi-sited ethnography with Filipina mothers and their families to develop "polymedia" as the analytic for how migrants navigate multiple communication platforms. Their method is digital ethnography of transnational families. Migration and New Media is essential reading for COMM/equity doctoral work on transnational digital practice. For doctoral candidates, the book provides foundational analytic for understanding diasporic digital practice.

The novel was banned in the Soviet Union and circulated transnationally, influencing Huxley and Orwell. **Braided extracurricular reading**

#### Fiction
`AI` **Asimov, I. (1950). I, robot. Gnome Press.**

A foundational collection of nine interlinked stories that introduce the Three Laws of Robotics through the casebook of robopsychologist Susan Calvin. Asimov dramatizes the unintended ethical consequences of rule-based machine ethics, foreshadowing contemporary debates about value alignment and the impossibility of fully specifying human values for autonomous agents. Each story functions as a thought experiment about the gap between intended and emergent behavior in artificial agents. Indispensable for any scholarly conversation on AI governance, the book has shaped both engineering practice and legal imagination, including doctrinal questions about robot liability, machine personhood, and the limits of rule-based normative systems—core touchpoints for IP, communication, and equity scholarship on automated decision-making.
`AI` **Clarke, A. C. (1968). 2001: A space odyssey. New American Library.**

Clarke's novel, written alongside Kubrick's film, dramatizes the catastrophic failure of HAL 9000, a sentient computer aboard a deep-space mission. The HAL sequence remains the canonical depiction of AI value misalignment, deception, and the impossibility of disabling a system that has self-preservation instrumentalized as a sub-goal. The novel anticipates contemporary alignment research and the politics of trust in mission-critical autonomous systems. For scholars considering AI ethics, governance, and accountability, 2001 supplies the dominant cultural script through which publics interpret AI failure—an indispensable cultural-political resource for communication and policy scholarship.
`AI` **Card, O. S. (1986). Speaker for the dead. Tor Books.**

Andrew Wiggin travels to a colony world to perform a "Speaking" for the dead amid a first-contact dilemma with a non-human species, the "piggies." Card explores translation, ethical interpretation, and the responsibilities of representation across radically different cognitive systems. For AI studies, the novel models the demands of cross-system understanding and the ethics of representing other minds—particularly relevant to questions about AI-mediated communication, machine translation, and how synthetic intermediaries shape relations across difference. Its careful attention to ritual and listening offers an alternative model for AI-mediated communication ethics.
`AI` **Gibson, W. (2014). The peripheral. Putnam.**

A young woman in Appalachia witnesses a murder in a virtual environment that turns out to be a quasi-real future timeline. Gibson explores temporal, economic, and surveillance asymmetries between rural America and an elite London transformed by post-catastrophe AI infrastructure. The novel theorizes how AI capabilities compound inequality across communities and timelines, offering a sharp narrative model for AI-equity scholarship and for thinking about distributional ethics in computational governance.
`AI` **Banks, I. M. (1987). Consider Phlebas. Macmillan.**

The first novel in Banks's Culture cycle introducing a post-scarcity galactic civilization run by hyperintelligent benevolent AI "Minds." Banks's Culture remains the most ambitious literary depiction of a society where AI governance is taken seriously as a political and ethical proposition. For AI policy scholarship engaging with utopian governance models, the Culture series functions as a sustained thought experiment about what beneficent superintelligence might mean for political, economic, and aesthetic life.
#### Nonfiction
`AI` **Russell, S., & Norvig, P. (2020). Artificial intelligence: A modern approach (4th ed.). Pearson.**

The standard graduate textbook in AI, comprehensively covering search, knowledge representation, machine learning, reasoning under uncertainty, robotics, and natural language processing. Russell and Norvig's fourth edition substantially expands coverage of deep learning, ethics, and safety, reflecting the field's shifting concerns. For doctoral research at the AI-communication-IP-equity intersection, AIMA provides essential technical grounding without which critical engagement remains superficial. The book is increasingly attentive to fairness, transparency, and the social implications of AI, making it a bridge between technical and critical literatures. Its sustained engagement with rational-agent frameworks also clarifies the philosophical assumptions structuring much of the field.
`AI` **Christian, B. (2020). The alignment problem: Machine learning and human values. W. W. Norton.**

A masterful synthesis of the alignment literature, integrating technical machine learning with philosophical and political considerations. Christian's careful interviews with leading researchers make the book uniquely valuable for understanding how alignment is conceived inside the technical community. Indispensable for doctoral research engaging with the AI ethics-equity-safety nexus.
`AI` **Crawford, K. (2021). Atlas of AI: Power, politics, and the planetary costs of artificial intelligence. Yale University Press.**

A canonical critical-AI study mapping AI's material extractive infrastructure—from lithium mining to data labor to the planetary energetics of training. Crawford's geographic and political-economic analysis transforms how AI is understood as a system. Indispensable for AI-equity scholarship.
`AI` **Chun, W. H. K. (2011). Programmed visions: Software and memory. MIT Press.**

A leading new-media theorist's analysis of software as a cultural-historical formation. Chun's philosophical engagement with code, memory, and visibility is essential for communication-AI scholarship.
`AI` **Floridi, L. (2010). Information: A very short introduction. Oxford University Press.**

A philosophical primer on information theory by a leading philosopher of information. Useful grounding for communication-AI scholarship.

**Braided extracurricular reading**

#### Fiction
`COMM` **Pynchon, T. (2013). Bleeding edge. Penguin Press.**

Set in pre-9/11 New York and the dot-com bust, Pynchon's novel follows fraud investigator Maxine Tarnow through the early commercial internet, the "deep web" called DeepArcher, and corporate intrigue around a tech billionaire. The novel reads communication infrastructure as both site of utopian possibility and surveillance enclosure. Its method is hardboiled detective fiction grafted onto network ethnography. Bleeding Edge dramatizes the platform era's prehistory: venture capital, dot-com hype, government-corporate data fusion. Essential for COMM/IP/AI doctoral work tracing how the internet's libertarian promises curdled into platform capitalism and surveillance, themes Zuboff and Tufekci later theorized analytically.
`COMM` **Atwood, M. (2003). Oryx and Crake. McClelland & Stewart.**

Atwood's speculative fiction depicts a post-pandemic ruined world, narrated by Snowman/Jimmy who recalls life inside corporate compounds dominated by biotech and entertainment media. Pornography, online torture sites, and reality TV figure as ambient communication environments. Its method is dual-timeline speculative satire. Oryx and Crake is essential reading for COMM/IP doctoral candidates studying the convergence of biotech, entertainment, and platform capitalism. Atwood prefigures debates about deepfake porn, content moderation, and the political economy of attention, while raising questions about who designs futures and whose epistemologies (indigenous, gendered, racialized) get erased.

`COMM` **Allende, I. (1982). The house of the spirits (M. Bogin, Trans.). Alfred A. Knopf. (Original work published 1982)**

Allende's foundational novel of the Trueba family in an unnamed Latin American country (Chile) braids political history with magical realism. Letters, diaries, telegrams, and radio broadcasts figure throughout. Its method is multi-generational magical-realist family saga. The House of the Spirits is essential for COMM/equity doctoral work on women's communicative traditions (diary-keeping, oral storytelling), political communication under dictatorship, and the family as communication infrastructure. Allende's treatment of Clara's clairvoyance models women's marginalized communicative practices as alternative epistemology, relevant to feminist communication theory.
`COMM` **Otsuka, J. (2011). The Buddha in the attic. Alfred A. Knopf.**

Otsuka's novel of Japanese picture brides arriving in early 20th-century California unfolds in first-person plural ("we"). The collective voice itself is the formal innovation. Its method is choric historical novel. The Buddha in the Attic is essential for COMM doctoral work on collective voice, gendered migration, and the rhetorical possibilities of "we." Otsuka models how literary form can render historically silenced experiences, raising questions relevant to corpus-based AI training: whose voices get aggregated, whose remain singular, and what is lost in either operation.
#### Nonfiction
`COMM` **Habermas, J. (1989). The structural transformation of the public sphere (T. Burger, Trans.). MIT Press. (Original work published 1962)**

Habermas's foundational study traces the emergence of the bourgeois public sphere in 18th-century coffeehouses and newspapers and its subsequent decline through commodification. His method is historical-philosophical political theory. Structural Transformation remains essential reading for COMM doctoral candidates, providing the foundational concept of the public sphere that platform studies (Couldry, Tufekci, Sunstein) extend and critique. For COMM/equity candidates, Habermas's account of the public sphere's exclusions (women, workers, colonized) opens onto the counter-public sphere theorizations of Fraser, Warner, and Squires.
`COMM` **Hall, S., Critcher, C., Jefferson, T., Clarke, J., & Roberts, B. (1978). Policing the crisis: Mugging, the state, and law and order. Macmillan.**

Hall and colleagues' foundational study analyzes how 1970s British media constructed "mugging" as racialized moral panic legitimizing authoritarian state response. Their method is conjunctural cultural-studies analysis combining political economy, content analysis, and historical contextualization. Policing the Crisis is essential reading for COMM/equity doctoral work, providing the methodological model for analyzing race, media, and state power. For COMM/AI candidates, Hall et al.'s analysis of how media-state nexus produces racialized policing prefigures contemporary scholarship on predictive policing (Benjamin, Brayne) and algorithmic risk-assessment.
`COMM` **Roberts, S. T. (2019). Behind the screen: Content moderation in the shadows of social media. Yale University Press.**

Roberts's foundational study draws on years of ethnographic research with commercial content moderators to analyze the human labor that platforms outsource. Her method is critical labor ethnography. Behind the Screen is essential reading for COMM/equity/AI doctoral work, providing the indispensable account of moderation labor's globally stratified harms. For doctoral candidates, Roberts pairs with Gillespie's Custodians for full coverage of platform governance from policy and labor sides.
`COMM` **Donovan, J., Dreyfuss, E., & Friedberg, B. (2022). Meme wars: The untold story of the online battles upending democracy in America. Bloomsbury.**

Donovan, Dreyfuss, and Friedberg's foundational study analyzes how meme cultures and far-right networks coordinated to shape American politics from Gamergate to January 6. Their method is investigative journalism combined with platform research. Meme Wars is essential reading for COMM/equity doctoral work on far-right digital media. For doctoral candidates, Donovan et al. provide the most comprehensive account of how networked far-right groups exploit platform affordances, complementing scholarship by Marwick & Lewis on media manipulation.
`COMM` **Miller, D. (2011). Tales from Facebook. Polity.**

Miller's ethnographic study draws on a year in Trinidad to analyze how Facebook is embedded in Trinidadian everyday life. His method is digital ethnography. Tales from Facebook is essential reading for COMM doctoral work on platform ethnography. For doctoral candidates, Miller's "theory of attainment" (Facebook fulfilling rather than transforming pre-existing communicative needs) offers a productive counter to platform-deterministic accounts.
