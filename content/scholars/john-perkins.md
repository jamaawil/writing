John Perkins is an American writer whose memoir Confessions of an Economic Hit Man claims that he worked as part of a covert system in which international financial consultants deliberately induced developing countries to accept inflated infrastructure loans they could never repay, in order to secure long-term political and economic leverage over those nations on behalf of American corporate and government interests. The book presents itself as an insider's exposé of how debt, rather than direct military conquest, became a primary tool of modern American economic empire, though its specific factual claims have been questioned by some critics given the memoir's lack of documentary verification. Regardless of the debate over its precise accuracy, the book became enormously influential in popularizing critiques of international debt and development finance as instruments of neocolonial control. Perkins has continued to write and speak on corporate power, debt, and what he calls the "corporatocracy." His work remains widely read as an entry point into critiques of international financial institutions' role in the Global South.
