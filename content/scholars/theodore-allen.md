Theodore Allen was an American historian and labor activist whose two-volume work The Invention of the White Race argued that "whiteness" as a racial category was not a natural or inevitable social division but a deliberately constructed political and legal category, invented specifically in colonial Virginia in the late seventeenth century to divide poor European indentured servants from enslaved Africans who had previously made common cause against colonial elites in rebellions like Bacon's Rebellion. He traced how colonial elites systematically extended new legal privileges to poor whites specifically to prevent cross-racial class solidarity, creating a durable racial hierarchy that served ruling-class interests by fracturing potential working-class unity. His scholarship, developed largely outside formal academic institutions through independent research and labor movement engagement, became highly influential in the development of critical whiteness studies. His argument that whiteness functions as a form of social control invented to serve ruling-class interests remains widely cited in labor history and critical race theory. He died in 2005, having spent much of his career as an independent scholar and labor activist.
