Nelson Mandela was a South African anti-apartheid revolutionary, lawyer, and statesman who spent twenty-seven years imprisoned, much of it on Robben Island, for his opposition to apartheid before his release in 1990 and subsequent election as South Africa's first Black president in 1994. His autobiography, Long Walk to Freedom, recounts his political awakening, his role in the African National Congress's shift toward armed resistance, his decades of imprisonment, and his central role in negotiating apartheid's peaceful end alongside his eventual former adversary F. W. de Klerk. His presidency emphasized racial reconciliation over retribution, embodied institutionally in the Truth and Reconciliation Commission, a stance that made him one of the most globally admired political figures of the twentieth century even as some critics later argued this approach left economic inequality from apartheid largely unaddressed. His life and writing remain central reference points for thinking about liberation struggle, imprisonment, forgiveness, and the possibilities and limits of negotiated transitions from authoritarian rule. He died in 2013, mourned globally as a singular figure in twentieth-century political history.
