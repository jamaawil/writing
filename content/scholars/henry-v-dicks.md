Henry V. Dicks was a British psychiatrist and a central figure in the early history of the Tavistock Clinic and Institute, whose book Fifty Years of the Tavistock Clinic chronicles the development of that pioneering institution's approach to psychoanalytic and group-oriented psychiatry from its founding through the mid-twentieth century. He was also known for his psychological research into the personalities of Nazi war criminals and ordinary German soldiers, seeking to understand the psychological conditions that enabled participation in atrocity, work that contributed to broader postwar debates about the psychology of obedience and violence. His institutional history situates the Tavistock's innovations in group therapy, marital therapy, and organizational consultation within the broader development of British psychoanalysis. He helped shape a distinctly British, socially oriented strand of psychoanalytic practice concerned with institutions and groups as much as individuals. His work remains a key historical source on the Tavistock tradition's origins.
