Zora Neale Hurston was an American writer and anthropologist, a major figure of the Harlem Renaissance, whose book Mules and Men presented the first substantial collection of African American folklore, drawing on her training in anthropology under Franz Boas to document oral storytelling traditions, hoodoo practices, and vernacular speech from Black communities in Florida and Louisiana with a rare combination of scholarly rigor and literary artistry. Her insistence on presenting Black folk culture in its own vernacular voice, without the condescension or exoticizing distance common in earlier folklore collection, was distinctive for its era and reflected her broader commitment to depicting Black life on its own terms rather than primarily in relation to white audiences or racial grievance. Her novel Their Eyes Were Watching God, largely overlooked at the time of publication, was later recovered as a landmark of American literature, particularly through the advocacy of writer Alice Walker. Her combined career in creative writing and anthropological fieldwork gave her folklore work a literary depth uncommon in academic ethnography of her period. She died in relative poverty and obscurity in 1960, before a major critical reappraisal restored her to recognition as one of the most significant American writers of the twentieth century.
