Eric Williams was a Trinidadian historian and statesman whose 1944 book Capitalism and Slavery advanced the enormously influential argument, now known as the Williams thesis, that the profits generated by Caribbean slavery and the slave trade were a crucial source of capital that fueled Britain's industrial revolution, and that British abolition came only once slavery had become economically less profitable rather than purely from humanitarian conviction. This challenged the prevailing narrative that credited abolition primarily to moral awakening, and it sparked decades of historical debate that continues among economic historians today. Trained at Oxford, Williams went on to become the first Prime Minister of independent Trinidad and Tobago, folding his historical scholarship into a career of anticolonial nation-building. His work remains foundational to the study of slavery's relationship to capitalism and to Caribbean historiography. The Williams thesis continues to shape debates in the "new history of capitalism."
