Khalil Gibran Muhammad is an American historian whose book The Condemnation of Blackness traces how, in the decades following the Civil War, statistics on crime were used to construct and reinforce the idea of Black Americans as inherently more criminal than white Americans, replacing older biological racist justifications for inequality with a supposedly objective, quantitative form of racist ideology. He shows how these early criminal statistics, often methodologically flawed and shaped by discriminatory policing practices, became embedded in policy and popular consciousness in ways that persist in the association of Blackness with criminality today. His work situates the origins of mass incarceration and racialized policing within this longer historical construction of Black criminality as statistical fact. He has held prominent public roles, including as director of the Schomburg Center for Research in Black Culture, alongside his academic position. His scholarship remains central to understanding the intellectual history of racialized crime statistics.
