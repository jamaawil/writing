Kwame Nkrumah was a Ghanaian statesman and Pan-African theorist who led Ghana to become the first sub-Saharan African nation to achieve independence from colonial rule in 1957, subsequently serving as its first prime minister and president. His book Neo-Colonialism: The Last Stage of Imperialism argued that formal political independence for African nations had not ended foreign domination but merely transformed it into a subtler form of economic and political control, in which former colonial powers and multinational corporations continued to dictate the terms of African economic life without the costs of direct colonial administration. This concept of neocolonialism became a central framework for understanding postcolonial African political economy and international relations more broadly. He was also a leading architect of Pan-Africanism as a political project, advocating for continental unity as a defense against continued foreign domination. He was deposed in a 1966 coup, reportedly with Western intelligence involvement, and his theoretical legacy continues to shape debates over sovereignty and dependency in the Global South.
