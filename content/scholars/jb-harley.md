J. B. Harley was a British geographer and historian of cartography whose work fundamentally reshaped how scholars understand maps, arguing in essays collected in volumes like The New Nature of Maps that maps are never objective, value-neutral representations of geographic reality but are instead social and political documents that encode the interests, silences, and power relationships of those who produce them. He introduced deconstructive and Foucauldian approaches to cartographic history, examining how colonial mapmaking, for instance, actively participated in claiming and reorganizing territory rather than simply recording it. His influential concept of maps as a form of power-knowledge helped found the field of critical cartography. He co-founded the monumental History of Cartography series, an ongoing multivolume scholarly project surveying mapmaking traditions across world cultures and periods. His theoretical interventions remain foundational to how historians and geographers now approach maps as cultural and political artifacts.
