Thomas Sankara was a Burkinabé revolutionary and president of Burkina Faso from 1983 until his assassination in 1987, whose speeches, collected as Thomas Sankara Speaks, articulated a radical vision of African self-reliance, anti-imperialism, and social transformation. During his brief tenure, he pursued ambitious programs of land reform, mass vaccination, women's rights, and reduced dependency on foreign aid, while renaming the country from Upper Volta to Burkina Faso, meaning "land of upright people," as a deliberate assertion of postcolonial identity distinct from its colonial French designation. His speeches combined uncompromising anti-imperialist rhetoric, including sharp criticism of international debt and Western aid dependency, with a distinctly personal, often humorous oratorical style that made him widely admired across Africa and the broader Global South. He was assassinated in a coup widely believed to have involved his close associate and successor Blaise Compaoré, with suspected French complicity, a killing that remains a subject of ongoing investigation and controversy. He remains an enduring symbol of radical African anti-imperialist politics and self-determination.
