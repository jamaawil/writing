Thomas Eisner was a German-American entomologist widely regarded as a founder of the field of chemical ecology, whose book For Love of Insects surveys decades of his research into the elaborate chemical defenses, communication systems, and behaviors that insects use to survive, from beetles that spray boiling defensive chemicals to moths that mimic the ultrasonic signatures of unpalatable species to deter bat predators. Writing for a general audience, he combined rigorous scientific description with genuine wonder at the sophistication of insect chemistry and behavior, arguing that insects deserved far greater scientific and public appreciation than their small size and common dismissal as pests would suggest. His research career, largely conducted at Cornell University, was foundational in establishing chemical ecology as a recognized scientific discipline. He was also an active conservation advocate, arguing that the vast, largely undocumented chemical diversity of insects represented an underappreciated resource facing extinction through habitat loss. His combination of rigorous science and accessible writing made him one of the most influential popularizers of entomology of his generation.
