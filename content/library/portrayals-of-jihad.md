> [!quote] Most Islamic scholars reject forced conversion through jihad [fact]
> Jihad has also been used in the context of conversion. This supports a common conception of Islam: that it is a religion of the sword. In fact, some scholars have expressed that jihad is legitimate in converting a non-Muslim to Islam. Ibn Khaldun, for example, believed that holy war is a religious duty of the Muslims “because of the universalism of the Muslim mission and (the obligation to) convert everybody to Islam either by persuasion or by force.” 13 However, most scholars of Islam (Egyptian scholars Mahmud Shaltut and Sayyid Qutb, for example) agree that jihad is never justified simply based on the goal of converting a non- Muslim. 14 This opinion stems from a verse in the Qur'an that states that there is no compulsion in religion. 15 They also point to other verses that seem to suggest Islam's acceptance of a diversity of religious beliefs.
>
> *XREF: Contrasts the 'religion of the sword' stereotype with scholarly consensus; connects to broader debates about Islam's stance on religious coercion.*

> [!quote] Jihad linguistically means striving, not holy war or fighting [definitional]
> For the most part, modern-day literature on jihad focuses on the greater jihad: the struggle to be a loyal servant of God, the struggle for the good of Muslim society and against corruption and decadence, etc. At the same time, almost all of these writings emphasize that there is a wider argument. They point out the linguistic derivation of the term “jihad” from jahada, which means “to exert oneself” or “to strive” and that this has a much friendlier connotation than the word qital, which means “to fight.” The latter necessarily implies killing or bloodshed, whereas jihad does not. 60 For this reason, “holy war” is not considered to be an accurate definition by modern-day scholars.
>
> *DEFINE: Clarifies the linguistic distinction between jihad (from jahada, to strive/exert) and qital (to fight, implying bloodshed), and why 'holy war' is rejected by modern scholars.*

<cite>Asena Karipek, Portrayals of Jihad, loc. 69</cite>

> [!quote] Jihad's etymology spans warfare, ego struggles, and service. [definitional]
> When we consider the etymology of jihad, the argument becomes very complex because the word “jihad” is incorporative of many different things including warfare, struggling with one’s ego, defending the oppressed, and even serving one’s parents. Therefore, to cherry-pick verses of the Qur’an and ahādīth to create a single, normative definition of jihad is irresponsible and inaccurate. Then, to use that definition to define Islam as a religion and spread Islamophobia based on that oversimplified/inaccurate definition, as Islamophobes (and to some extent, the American media and politicians) do, is to misunderstand the religion and to do injustice to the 1.8 billion adherents to this faith worldwide.
