The unbroken line from Athens to Washington is a modern fabrication that retroactively claims Greek thought while erasing Islamic, African, and Asian transmission — assembled to justify supremacy.

# Chapter One
## The Golden Nugget
### How "Western Civilization" Was Invented, and When

In the nine villages around Umuofia, everyone knew Okonkwo's name before they knew his face. He had made it himself, at eighteen, by throwing Amalinze the Cat — the wrestler who for seven years had never let his back touch the earth. The drums beat, the flutes sang, and the fight was still spoken of twenty years later. Okonkwo grew into a man of titles and barns and three wives, a man the clan measured itself against, his fame spreading through the nine villages and beyond them.

> [!quote] Chinua Achebe, *Things Fall Apart*
> Okonkwo was well known throughout the nine villages and even beyond. His fame rested on solid personal achievements. As a young man of eighteen he had brought honor to his village by throwing Amalinze the Cat. Amalinze was the great wrestler who for seven years was unbeaten, from Umuofia to Mbaino. He was called the Cat because his back would never touch the earth. It was this man that Okonkwo threw in a fight which the old men agreed was one of the fiercest since the founder of their town engaged a spirit of the wild for seven days and seven nights.

None of this required Europe's permission. It did not wait for a ship, a missionary, or a map. By the time Chinua Achebe sat down in the 1950s to write *Things Fall Apart*, the Igbo world he was describing — its law, its proverbs, its wrestling matches, its gods, its arguments about honor and cowardice and what a man owes his ancestors — had already been running for centuries on its own clock, according to its own logic, answerable to no one outside it. Achebe did not invent that world to flatter it. He gave his people a civilization complex enough to contain its own tragedies, its own Okonkwo, undone in the end as much by his own rigidity as by the empire that arrives, almost gently at first, in the novel's final pages.

I start here, in Umuofia, because it is not where the story of "Western civilization" is supposed to start. That story has a different opening scene, and most of us can recite it without having to think very hard: Athens, its marble columns and its philosophers arguing in the agora; then Rome, inheriting the torch and building roads to carry it; then a jump — vague, unquestioned — to Paris and London, the cathedrals and the parliaments; and finally a boat across the Atlantic to Washington, where the torch supposedly still burns. A straight line. A relay race. A family tree with exactly one trunk.

It is almost entirely fabricated. And the person who has done the most rigorous work of dismantling it, brick by brick, is not a radical outsider but a philosopher who holds an endowed chair at NYU: Kwame Anthony Appiah — the same Appiah who, elsewhere, wrote the foreword to Achebe's own collected novels. In a chapter of his book *The Lies That Bind: Rethinking Identity*, he gives the myth a name that is hard to forget once you've heard it: the golden nugget.

---

**The Golden Nugget**

Appiah's version of the myth goes like this. Somewhere in antiquity, a precious substance was mined — the best of Greek thought and Roman order. The Greeks dug it up. Rome conquered Greece and inherited it. From there, according to the story, it traveled intact through the centuries, gathering shine, until it arrived, whole, in the modern West.

> [!quote] Kwame Anthony Appiah, *The Lies That Bind*
> So from the late Middle Ages through Hegel until now, people have thought of the best in the culture of Greece and Rome as a European inheritance, passed on like a precious golden nugget, dug out of the earth by the Greeks, and transferred, when the Roman Empire conquered them, to Rome, where it got a good polish. Eventually, it was partitioned among the Flemish and Florentine courts and the Venetian Republic in the Renaissance, its fragments passing through cities such as Avignon, Paris, Amsterdam, Weimar, Edinburgh, and London, and finally reunited in the academies of Europe and the United States. This priceless treasure is no doubt nestled now somewhere here in the American academy, where I work . . . perhaps in the university library right around the corner.

The joke lands because the story is so familiar it barely registers as a story at all. It feels less like an argument than a fact of geology. But Appiah traces its actual age, and it is not ancient — it is medieval public relations. At the end of the twelfth century, the French poet Chrétien de Troyes wrote a version of it himself:

> [!quote] Chrétien de Troyes, quoted in *The Lies That Bind*
> Greece once had the greatest reputation for chivalry and learning. Then chivalry went to Rome, and so did all of learning, which now has come to France.

Six hundred years later, on the other side of a continent and a Reformation, Hegel told the students at the German high school he ran something almost identical:

> [!quote] G. W. F. Hegel, quoted in *The Lies That Bind*
> The foundation of higher study must be and remain Greek literature in the first place, Roman in the second.

Different centuries, different languages, the same claim: *we are the rightful, exclusive heirs.* What neither man was doing was describing a real, unbroken chain of custody. Chrétien de Troyes was writing for an audience that mostly could not read Latin, let alone Greek — feudal subjects with no daily connection whatsoever to the Athens they were being told they'd inherited. The golden nugget was never dug up and handed down like an heirloom watch. It was buried, lost, forgotten by the people now claiming it, and recovered — this is the part the myth leaves out — almost entirely somewhere else.

That somewhere else was Baghdad. In the eighth and ninth centuries, under the Abbasid caliphs, the palace library known as the Bayt al-Hikmah — the House of Wisdom — became the site of one of history's great intellectual undertakings: the systematic translation of Plato, Aristotle, Pythagoras, and Euclid into Arabic. Appiah is precise about what this means for the golden-nugget story:

> [!quote] Kwame Anthony Appiah, *The Lies That Bind*
> In the centuries that Petrarch called the Dark Ages, when Christian Europe made little contribution to the study of Greek classical philosophy, and many of the texts were lost to view, these works, and the capacity to interpret them, were preserved by Muslim scholars. And a good deal of what we now know of the texts of classical philosophy and how to read them we know only because that knowledge was recovered by Europe . . . from the Arabic.

Scholars writing in Arabic — building a tradition they called *falsafa* — did not simply guard the texts like museum pieces. They argued with Aristotle, extended him, invented algebra, advanced astronomy. When Christian Europe finally wanted him back, it could not just pull him off a shelf. European scholars had to translate him out of Arabic, because the Greek originals were gone and the language itself had been lost. In Córdoba — Islamic Spain, its streets paved and lit at night centuries before London's — the Muslim jurist and philosopher Ibn Rushd wrote commentaries on Aristotle so extensive that medieval University of Paris scholars simply called him "the Commentator," as though there could be no other. A Muslim philosopher from Spain became the lens through which Christian Europe rediscovered its own supposed founding father. That is not a straight line. That is a debt.

---

**How the West Was Spun**

Here is the detail that tends to stop people mid-sentence: the word "West," used the way we use it now — as the name of a civilization, a heritage, an "us" — is not ancient at all. Appiah puts a date on it:

> [!quote] Kwame Anthony Appiah, *The Lies That Bind*
> In English, the very idea of the "West," to name a heritage and object of study, doesn't really emerge until the 1880s and 1890s, during a heated era of imperialism, and gains broader currency only in the twentieth century.

The 1880s and 1890s were not a quiet decade of disinterested scholarship. They were the decade of the Berlin Conference, when European powers sat around a table and partitioned an entire continent among themselves without a single African representative present. "The West" as an identity — a shared inheritance uniting London and Lisbon and, soon, Washington — was coined at almost exactly the moment those same powers needed a story that explained why they, and not the people whose land they were taking, were the ones entitled to rule.

The concept was reinforced again in the middle of the twentieth century, for a different but related reason. The Cold War needed a unifying story, and Appiah names what got forged in the process:

> [!quote] Kwame Anthony Appiah, *The Lies That Bind*
> If the notion of Christendom was an artifact of a prolonged series of military struggles against Muslim forces, our modern concept of Western culture largely took its present shape in the late 1940s and the 1950s, during the Cold War. In the chill of battle, we forged a grand Plato-to-NATO narrative about Athenian democracy, the Magna Carta, the Copernican Revolution, and so on. Western culture was, at its core, individualistic and democratic and liberty-minded and tolerant and progressive and rational and scientific. Never mind that premodern Europe was none of these things, and that until the past century democracy was the exception in Europe, something that few stalwarts of Western thought had anything good to say about. The idea that tolerance was constitutive of something called Western culture would certainly have surprised Edward Burnett Tylor, who, as a Quaker, had been barred from attending England's great universities.

This is what Appiah calls the fallacy of organicism: the assumption that a culture is a single, seamless whole, so that if you have Athenian philosophy, Western democracy and Western tolerance must follow naturally, like organs adapted to their place in one body.

> [!quote] Kwame Anthony Appiah, *The Lies That Bind*
> What you're hearing there is something we can call organicism. A vision of culture not as a loose assemblage of disparate fragments but as an organic unity, each component, like the organs in a body, carefully adapted to occupy a particular place, each part essential to the functioning of the whole.

The historical record refuses to cooperate with this picture:

> [!quote] Kwame Anthony Appiah, *The Lies That Bind*
> Spain, in the heart of the West, resisted liberal democracy for two generations after it took off in India and Japan in the East, the home of Oriental despotism. Jefferson's cultural inheritance — Athenian liberty, Anglo-Saxon freedom — did not prevent the United States from creating a slave republic.

If democracy and tolerance were organic features of Western civilization, passed down like a genetic trait, the timeline makes no sense — Spain a dictatorship into the 1970s, decades after "Eastern" India and Japan had already built functioning democracies; a founding father who could write of liberty and hold hundreds of people in bondage at the same time, in the same body, in the same nation. The pieces were never welded together into one organism. They are separate choices, sitting side by side, sometimes in flat contradiction.

---

**The Uses of a Myth**

None of this is academic hairsplitting, and Appiah does not pretend it is. He follows the golden-nugget myth to where it is still doing damage today:

> [!quote] Kwame Anthony Appiah, *The Lies That Bind*
> In some of the darker recesses of the Internet, enthusiasts for the idea of North America or Europe as the home of the White Race celebrate the achievements they claim for the West as somehow theirs. They claim National Socialism and Shakespeare, eugenics and Euclid, democracy and Dante.

These groups lean on the German concept of *Geist* — a racial essence, a spirit, supposedly passed down through blood and soil, connecting a modern white American to Euclid's mathematics or Shakespeare's sonnets by nothing more than a shared, imagined ancestry. Appiah's rebuttal is total:

> [!quote] Kwame Anthony Appiah, *The Lies That Bind*
> Yet if they believe that something in them, some racial essence, somehow connects them with an organic kernel, a Geist, that pervades Western culture, they understand neither race nor civilization.

You do not inherit a love of liberty through your blood any more than you inherit a language through it. Nobody is born fluent. So if values aren't a birthright, what are they? Appiah answers with a story about beads:

> [!quote] Kwame Anthony Appiah, *The Lies That Bind*
> For centuries, the people on the Venetian island of Murano made a living because glassmakers there perfected their useful art. Their beads, with multicolored filaments, some made of gold, were among the artistic wonders of the world. To keep their commercial advantage, the Venetian state forbade glassmakers from leaving with their secrets; the penalty for revealing them to outsiders was death. Good for Murano and its profits: bad for everyone else . . . Venetian beads were already being imported into the Gold Coast by the turn of the seventeenth century, arriving across the Sahara.

The people who received those beads in West Africa did not display them as curiosities of European genius. They melted them down and remade them into their own ornamental traditions, on their own terms. The glass no longer belonged to Murano. It never really had. This is Appiah's answer to the golden nugget: culture does not move like an inheritance, passed intact from a single origin down a single line to a single set of rightful heirs. It moves like those beads — traded, melted, remade, claimed by whoever actually uses it, wherever they happen to be standing. A student in Lagos or Nairobi who has genuinely studied Plato has more claim to that inheritance than a person born in London who has never opened the book. Geography grants nothing. Ancestry grants nothing. The values have to be chosen, and practiced, over and over — or, as Appiah puts it at the very end of the chapter, they are just borrowed light from a source no one bothered to visit.

---

**The Wound Underneath**

Appiah's golden nugget explains how the myth of an unbroken European inheritance was assembled and who it was assembled to flatter. But there is a second, harder question underneath it, one that the Argentine-Mexican philosopher Enrique Dussel presses further in *The Invention of the Americas*: why did this myth need to exist at all? What was it covering for?

Dussel's answer runs through a single date: 1492. Not as the beginning of a voyage of discovery, but as what he calls the origin myth of modernity itself — the year Europe's sense of its own centrality was not just imagined but *enforced*, on bodies, on land, on entire continents whose own histories were, in that instant, declared to have ended. Where Appiah dismantles the myth's genealogy, Dussel and the semiotician Walter Mignolo ask what the myth licensed people to do once they believed it: to draw a line through time itself, and act as though everything on the far side of that line — every law, every calendar, every god that existed in the Americas before 1492 — had simply stopped counting.

That is where this book goes next. Because the golden nugget was never only a story Europe told about itself. It was also, always, a story told *about* everyone it excluded — and those people did not stay silent about it. Some of them picked up the very books, the very language, the very Shakespeare that had been used to justify their conquest, and wrote back. In Umuofia, in Córdoba's mirror image, in a Kenyan village on the eve of independence, in a fishing town on the Nile — the line that was supposed to run straight from Athens to Washington turns out to pass through a great many places it was never supposed to acknowledge.

The rest of this book goes to find them.

# Chapter Two
## The Wound of 1492
### How Conquest, Not Philosophy, Began the Modern World

Enrique Dussel wrote the preface to *The Invention of the Americas* in Seville, and he could not help but notice where he was sitting. This had been the land of the Moors — Muslim Spain, the tail end of the caliphate that had once made Córdoba, a few hours up the river, one of the most sophisticated cities on earth. That world ended on a specific day. On January 6, 1492, the last sultan of Granada, Boabdil, handed the keys of his city to the Catholic kings. Eight centuries of Islamic Iberia closed in an afternoon.

Most people, if they remember 1492 at all, remember a different event from later that same year — three ships, an ocean, a "discovery." Dussel's argument is that these two events, the fall of Granada in January and Columbus's landfall in October, are not a coincidence sharing a calendar. They are the same event, wearing two faces. The same crown that finished conquering the Moors immediately turned its fleet west and began conquering everyone else. Dussel does not treat 1492 as the start of an age of exploration. He treats it as a birth — the birth of something he simply calls modernity, and he is exact about the date.

> [!quote] Enrique Dussel, *The Invention of the Americas*
> The birthdate of modernity is 1492, even though its gestation, like that of the fetus, required a period of intrauterine growth. Whereas modernity gestated in the free, creative medieval European cities, it came to birth in Europe's confrontation with the Other. By controlling, conquering, and violating the Other, Europe defined itself as discoverer, conquistador, and colonizer of an alterity likewise constitutive of modernity. Europe never discovered (des-cubierto) this Other as Other but covered over (encubierto) the Other as part of the Same: i.e., Europe.

That last sentence carries the whole chapter. *Des-cubrir*, in Spanish, is to un-cover, to discover. Dussel's pun is that Europe did the opposite. It did not uncover the Americas and meet what was actually there. It covered the Americas over — draped its own categories across an entire hemisphere of nations, languages, calendars, and cosmologies, and then declared that hemisphere to have been empty, or savage, or waiting, until a European ship arrived to give it meaning. The "discovery," in other words, was itself the first act of erasure. You cannot discover a place and simultaneously deny that anyone was already living there in full possession of a history. Dussel's whole point is that Europe did both, at the same moment, and called it the beginning of the modern world.

---

**Europe's Alibi**

If modernity was born in violence, it needed, almost immediately, a story that made the violence look like something else — an explanation for why Europe, and not the societies it was conquering, was the one entitled to sit at the center of history. Dussel finds that alibi already fully formed in the work of the sociologist Max Weber, who opened his study of world history by asking a question that assumes its own answer before a word of evidence is offered:

> [!quote] Max Weber, quoted in Dussel's *The Invention of the Americas*
> Which chain of circumstances has resulted in the fact that on Western soil and only there cultural phenomena have been produced which, as we represent it, show signs of evolutionary advance and universal validity?

Notice the shape of the question. It does not ask *whether* Europe alone produced universal knowledge. It asks only *how*. Dussel calls this move Eurocentric, and he does not mean it as a vague insult — he means that Weber has quietly written Europe into the center of the map before the investigation even begins, the same way a rigged experiment writes its conclusion into its method. And when Dussel checks the actual history behind Weber's claim, it collapses on contact with the same fact this book has already spent a chapter establishing:

> [!quote] Enrique Dussel, *The Invention of the Americas*
> He argues that science rose in the West in contrast to India and China, but he forgets the Islamic world from which the Latin Occident, in particular the Oxford Franciscans and the Paduan Marsilios, learned Aristotelianism and the experiential, empirical attitude. One could easily falsify Weber's Hellenic, Eurocentric arguments by taking 1492 as the ultimate example of the pretended superiority of the Occident over other cultures.

The Oxford Franciscans — the medieval English monks whose scholarship is routinely cited as the seedbed of European empirical science — did not develop their method in a vacuum. They inherited it, the same way Aquinas inherited Aristotle: through the Arabic philosophical tradition, the same tradition this book has already traced back to Baghdad's House of Wisdom and Córdoba's court of Ibn Rushd. Weber's account of European exceptionalism requires forgetting a debt that was, by any honest reading, enormous.

---

**Colonizing Time**

Dussel explains *when* modernity claimed to begin and *what alibi* it used to justify what followed. The Argentine semiotician Walter Mignolo, in *The Darker Side of Western Modernity*, asks a stranger and more unsettling question: what, exactly, did Europe have to do to the rest of the world's sense of *time itself* in order to make that claim stick?

Mignolo's answer is that Europe did not simply conquer land in 1492. It reorganized history so that everything which existed before European contact — no matter how old, how vast, how internally sophisticated — was reclassified as *before*, as prelude, as a rough draft awaiting the real story.

> [!quote] Walter Mignolo, *The Darker Side of Western Modernity*
> "Western modernity" has been an addition and also an enormous contribution to the many histories of cultures and civilizations that have appeared since homo erectus began to walk around the planet . . . But it by no means should be taken as the point of arrival of human existence on the planet. The history of Western civilization was conceived in the period between the Renaissance and the Enlightenment . . . It was during the Renaissance that the invention of the Middle Ages and the invention of America appropriated the idea of history, colonized time, and space and located Europe as the point of reference of global history.

Read that twice: the invention of the Middle Ages and the invention of America happened together, as a single maneuver. To call a period the "Middle Ages" is to declare it a hallway between two more important rooms — a container for time that doesn't count on its own terms. Europe performed the identical operation on an entire hemisphere. The Aztec calendar, the Inca road system, the political order of the Haudenosaunee — none of it was absorbed into a shared human timeline as a parallel, coequal history. It was filed under "before," the same way the Middle Ages were filed under "before," waiting for modernity to arrive and make it count.

Mignolo traces the deeper mechanics of this move through the German legal theorist Carl Schmitt, who spent his career studying how nations draw the invisible lines — the *nomos* — that organize a world into order and disorder, inside and outside, history and not-yet-history. Schmitt himself, writing in the 1950s, located the hinge of world history at almost the same year Dussel does:

> [!quote] Carl Schmitt, quoted in Mignolo's *The Darker Side of Western Modernity*
> There always has been some kind of nomos of the earth. In all the ages of mankind, the earth has been appropriated, divided and cultivated. But before the age of the great discoveries, before the sixteenth century of our system of dating, men had no global concept of the planet on which they lived. Certainly, they had a mythical image of heaven and earth, and of land and sea, but the earth still was not measured as a globe, and men still had not ventured onto the great oceans.

Mignolo agrees with the facts and rejects the frame. Yes, a genuinely planetary map — one ocean connecting all shores — did not exist before European navigators pieced it together in the sixteenth century. But Schmitt's sentence performs, quietly, the exact erasure Mignolo is diagnosing: it treats "no global concept of the planet" as equivalent to no order, no *nomos*, no legitimate world at all — a "mythical image," not a real one. Mignolo's counter is not that Europe's cartographers were lying about their achievement. It's that a world does not have to be mapped by Europe to have existed, governed itself, and mattered on its own terms. The Aztec and Inca *nomos* were not absent before 1492. They were simply not consulted before Europe declared the planet, for the first time, officially whole.

---

**What the Myth Was For**

Put Dussel and Mignolo side by side and Chapter One's golden nugget looks different than it did a chapter ago. Appiah showed that "the West" is a story assembled after the fact, its lineage far more borrowed and far less pure than the myth admits. Dussel and Mignolo answer the question the myth was built to avoid: borrowed from whom, and at what cost. The golden nugget was never just an origin story. It was cover — *encubrimiento* — for the fact that modernity's actual birth certificate is dated 1492 and signed in conquest, not philosophy.

That conquest did not stay on the far side of the Atlantic. The same logic that declared an entire hemisphere's history void also went looking for what it considered disorder much closer to home — in the villages of Europe itself, and especially in the bodies of European women. That is where this book goes next.

# Chapter Three
## The Bonfires That Built the Bank
### Witch Hunts and the Making of Capitalism

Before there was a fence, there was a commons. A serf in medieval Europe owed labor to a lord — real, compulsory, unglamorous labor on the lord's own land — but in exchange for it, something crucial passed the other way. The feminist historian Silvia Federici, in *Caliban and the Witch*, is precise about what that exchange actually secured:

> [!quote] Silvia Federici, *Caliban and the Witch*
> The most important aspect of serfdom, from the viewpoint of the changes it introduced in the master-servant relation, is that it gave the serfs direct access to the means of their reproduction. In exchange for the work which they were bound to do on the lords' land, the serfs received a plot of land which they could use to support themselves.

That plot of land came bundled with something even more valuable: the commons. Meadows, forests, lakes, wild pastures, shared ground that belonged to no one and to everyone — wood for fuel, fish, grazing, timber for building.

> [!quote] Silvia Federici, *Caliban and the Witch*
> With the use of land also came the use of the "commons" — meadows, forests, lakes, wild pastures — that provided crucial resources for the peasant economy and fostered community cohesion and cooperation.

This is not a nostalgic picture of feudalism, which was still built on coercion and hierarchy. It is a narrower, more specific point: a peasant with access to a commons cannot be starved into submission quite as easily as a peasant with none. If you can graze a cow, cut your own firewood, and fish a shared lake, you are never entirely at the mercy of a wage, because you are never entirely dependent on anyone paying you one. Federici's argument in *Caliban and the Witch* is that this baseline of subsistence was not gradually and peacefully phased out by the arrival of a more efficient market economy. It was taken. On purpose. Because as long as it existed, capitalism could not get off the ground — nobody willingly walks into a textile mill for pennies a day if they can still feed themselves on the commons.

---

**A Counterrevolution**

This is the detail that inverts the textbook story: Federici does not treat the rise of capitalism as a revolution against feudal tyranny, the merchant class throwing off the chains of kings and lords. She treats it as the opposite — a *counter*revolution, a violent correction against a peasantry that, in the wake of the Black Death, had briefly gained the upper hand. With labor suddenly scarce after the plague swept Europe, peasants had leverage for the first time in generations: they refused feudal dues, organized communally, and in places came close to genuine, self-governed egalitarianism. The ruling class did not respond by negotiating. It responded by taking the land back through what historians call the enclosures — fencing off common ground, criminalizing the trespass of people who had grazed and gathered there for generations, turning subsistence farmers into a landless, desperate population with nothing left to sell but their own labor. Manufactured scarcity, in other words, engineered on a continental scale, to build the one thing early capitalism actually needed: a workforce with no alternative but the wage.

That workforce still had to be controlled, though — kept in line, kept producing, kept from remembering what had just been done to it. And this is where Federici's book earns its title. Because the tool the state reached for was not a new one. It was witchcraft.

---

**The Witch as Economic Policy**

The popular image of the witch trials is medieval: torch-lit villages, illiterate peasants, the "Dark Ages" at their most superstitious. Federici's book spends considerable energy dismantling exactly that image, because the actual chronology runs the opposite direction. The mass European witch hunts did not happen in the early medieval period — they exploded in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, the same era being celebrated elsewhere as the dawn of the Scientific Revolution.

> [!quote] Silvia Federici, *Caliban and the Witch*
> In this "century of geniuses" — Bacon, Kepler, Galileo, Shakespeare, Pascal, Descartes — a century that saw the triumph of the Copernican Revolution, the birth of modern science, and the development of philosophical and scientific rationalism, witchcraft became one of the favorite subjects of debate for the European intellectual elites.

Judges, lawyers, and theologians — not frightened villagers — wrote the treatises, and the newly invented printing press carried them across the continent. And the purpose, according to Federici, was never really theological. It was structural:

> [!quote] Silvia Federici, *Caliban and the Witch*
> The witch-hunt was one of the most important events in the development of capitalist society and the formation of the modern proletariat. For the unleashing of a campaign of terror against women, unmatched by any other persecution, weakened the resistance of the European peasantry to the assault launched against it by the gentry and the state.

A charge of witchcraft was vague enough to be aimed at almost anyone who threatened the new order: the healer with generational knowledge of herbs and contraception, the disobedient wife, the widow who inherited land, the midwife who knew how to end a pregnancy the state now needed carried to term. Village women had long functioned as the informal health care system of their communities — control over birth, healing, and reproduction concentrated in women's hands, outside anyone's official authority. A newly landless, wage-dependent economy needed exactly the opposite: bodies that reliably reproduced a future labor supply, on a schedule the state could rely on. Terrorizing the women who held that older, uncontrolled knowledge and transferring their authority to an emerging, state-licensed male medical profession was not a side effect of the witch hunts. It was close to the entire point.

---

**A Body Made of Brute Matter**

None of this required abandoning reason — it required *redefining* it. The same century that burned women as witches also produced the philosophical framework of mechanical philosophy, the idea, advanced by Descartes and others, that only the human mind possesses anything like a soul, while the body itself is closer to a machine.

> [!quote] Silvia Federici, *Caliban and the Witch*
> In Mechanical Philosophy, the body is described by analogy with the machine, often with emphasis on its inertia. The body is conceived as brute matter, wholly divorced from any rational qualities: it does not know, does not want, does not feel.

If a body is nothing but a collection of parts — unfeeling, unthinking, "brute matter" — then working it fourteen hours a day in a mill carries no more moral weight than running any other machine into the ground. Federici's point is that the metaphysics and the economics were not two separate developments that happened to share a century. The philosophy supplied the license the new labor system needed.

---

**The Trip Across the Atlantic**

None of this stayed at home. The same missionaries and colonial administrators who had just perfected the rhetoric of witchcraft in European villages carried it across the ocean, where it did identical work: any Indigenous healer, any spiritual leader, any woman who held visible authority in her community could be, and routinely was, denounced using the same vocabulary already tested on the European peasantry. The enclosures that had fenced off English commons became, on a hemispheric scale, the seizure of land already explored in the last chapter — the same operating logic, exported and enlarged.

Federici's deepest claim is that this was never a coincidence of timing. Capitalism did not stumble into sexism and racism as unfortunate holdovers from a less enlightened past, destined to fade as markets modernized. It required them. A system that depends on someone's labor going unpaid — a wife's reproductive labor, a colonized subject's stolen land and body — needs a standing justification for why that person doesn't count as fully entitled to a wage or a right in the first place. The bonfires in the village square and the ledgers in the counting house were never in different histories. They were financing each other.

The next place this same machinery went looking for a population it could define, classify, and rule was not across the Atlantic but to the east — toward an entire civilization Europe needed to invent almost from scratch, in order to have something to call itself against.
